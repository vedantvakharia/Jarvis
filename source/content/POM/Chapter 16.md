## 16.1 What is Motivation?

**Motivation** - the process by which a person's efforts are energized, directed, and sustained toward attaining a goal.

Three key elements:
- **Energy** - measure of intensity, drive, and vigor; quality of effort matters, not just quantity
- **Direction** - effort must be channeled toward organizational goals, not just any effort
- **Persistence** - sustained effort over time to achieve goals

> Common misconception: motivation is NOT a personal trait (i.e., "some people are motivated, others aren't"). Motivational drive varies by situation - you may be more motivated in some contexts than others.

**Why it matters:** Gallup Poll found ~68% of US employees are disengaged. Globally, ~87% are not excited about their work.

## 16.2 Early Theories of Motivation

Four foundational theories - not empirically strong, but historically important and still widely used by managers.

---

### 1. Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs Theory

Proposed by Abraham Maslow. Five needs arranged in a hierarchy:

| Level | Need Type | Description |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| 5 (top) | Self-Actualization | Growth, achieving potential, self-fulfillment |
| 4 | Esteem | Self-respect, autonomy, achievement, status, recognition |
| 3 | Social | Affection, belongingness, acceptance, friendship |
| 2 | Safety | Security, protection from physical and emotional harm |
| 1 (base) | Physiological | Food, drink, shelter, sex, other physical needs |

**Key principles:**
- Each level must be **substantially satisfied** before the next becomes dominant
- Lower-order needs (physiological, safety) - satisfied externally
- Higher-order needs (social, esteem, self-actualization) - satisfied internally
- Once a need is substantially satisfied, it no longer motivates

**Managerial implication:** Identify where an employee is in the hierarchy; focus on satisfying needs at or above that level.

**Criticism:** Maslow provided **no empirical support**. Multiple studies failed to validate it. Widely popular in the 1960s-70s due to intuitive appeal, not evidence.

---

### 2. McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y

Douglas McGregor proposed two contrasting assumptions about human nature:

**Theory X (negative view):**
- Employees dislike work
- Are lazy
- Avoid responsibility
- Must be coerced, controlled, threatened to perform

**Theory Y (positive view):**
- Employees enjoy work
- Seek out and accept responsibility
- Exercise self-direction
- Are creative

McGregor believed Theory Y should guide management practice. Proposed that participation in decision making, responsible/challenging jobs, and good group relations maximize motivation.

**Criticism:** No evidence confirms either set of assumptions is universally valid. Effective managers using Theory X assumptions exist in practice (e.g., highly directive but successful leaders). Theory Y is not the only valid approach.

---

### 3. Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory (Motivation-Hygiene Theory)

Frederick Herzberg studied what made people feel exceptionally good or bad about their jobs.

**Key finding:** Factors causing satisfaction are *different* from factors causing dissatisfaction - they are not opposites on a single continuum.

#### Two Separate Continua:

**Motivators** (intrinsic - related to job itself) -> lead to satisfaction:
- Achievement
- Recognition
- Work itself
- Responsibility
- Advancement
- Growth

**Hygiene Factors** (extrinsic - related to job context) -> when absent, cause dissatisfaction:
- Supervision
- Company policy
- Relationship with supervisor
- Working conditions
- Salary
- Relationship with peers
- Personal life
- Relationship with subordinates
- Status
- Security

#### Herzberg's View vs. Traditional View:

| Traditional | Herzberg |
|-------------|----------|
| Satisfied <------> Dissatisfied (one scale) | Satisfaction <-> No Satisfaction (motivators) |
| | No Dissatisfaction <-> Dissatisfaction (hygiene factors) |

**Critical insight:** Removing hygiene problems (e.g., fixing bad pay) eliminates dissatisfaction but does NOT create motivation. To motivate, you must improve the intrinsic motivators.

**Implication:** Managers who only fix hygiene issues are playing defense, not offense.

**Criticism:** Methodology was criticized for being too simplistic. Despite this, it heavily influenced **job enrichment** practices and modern job design.

---

### 4. McClelland's Three-Needs Theory

David McClelland: three **acquired** (not innate) needs are major work motives:

**1. Need for Achievement (nAch)**
- Drive to succeed and excel relative to a set of standards
- Prefer personal responsibility for problem-solving
- Want rapid, unambiguous feedback on performance
- Set moderately challenging goals (avoid extremes - too easy or too hard)
- High nAch does NOT necessarily make a good manager in large orgs - high achievers focus on *own* accomplishments, whereas good managers focus on *others'* goals
- Can be trained/developed

**2. Need for Power (nPow)**
- Need to make others behave in ways they otherwise wouldn't
- Best managers tend to be **high nPow, low nAff**

**3. Need for Affiliation (nAff)**
- Desire for friendly and close interpersonal relationships
- High nAff managers tend to be less effective (let relationships override organizational needs)

**Measurement:** Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) - respondents write stories based on ambiguous pictures; trained interpreters assess nAch, nPow, nAff levels from the stories.

**Note:** nAch research is most extensive. nPow and nAff are less studied.

## 16.3 Contemporary Theories of Motivation

Better supported by research than early theories. Include: Goal-Setting, Reinforcement, Job Design (JCM), Equity, and Expectancy theories.

---

### 1. Goal-Setting Theory

**Core proposition:** Specific and difficult goals, when accepted, result in higher performance than easy goals or "do your best" goals.

**Key findings:**
- Specific goals act as internal stimuli
- Challenging goals produce higher output than vague goals
- Goals must be *accepted* by the employee to drive performance

**Reconciling with nAch:** Goal-setting theory applies to people in general; nAch applies specifically to high achievers (~10-20% of population). Difficult goals are still recommended for the majority.

**Participation in goal-setting:**
- Not always superior to assigned goals
- Participation is preferable when employees might resist difficult challenges

**Feedback:**
- Necessary to identify gaps between current and desired performance
- **Self-generated feedback** is a more powerful motivator than externally provided feedback

**Three contingency factors:**

| Factor | Details |
|--------|---------|
| Goal commitment | Most likely when goals are public, individual has internal locus of control, goals are self-set |
| Self-efficacy | Belief in one's ability to perform; high self-efficacy -> tries harder under difficulty; responds to negative feedback with increased effort |
| National culture | Goal-setting aligns best with cultures low in power distance, low in uncertainty avoidance, high in assertiveness (e.g., North America) |

**Bottom line:** Hard, specific goals + commitment + feedback + high self-efficacy = higher performance. But hard goals are NOT associated with increased job *satisfaction*.

---

### 2. Reinforcement Theory

Based on B.F. Skinner's operant conditioning.

**Core proposition:** Behavior is a function of its consequences. Reinforcers are consequences that immediately follow a behavior and increase its probability of repetition.

**Key principles:**
- Rewarded behaviors are repeated
- Rewards most effective when they *immediately* follow desired behavior
- Ignored or punished behaviors are less likely to be repeated

**Punishment vs. nonreinforcement:**
- Punishment eliminates undesired behavior faster
- BUT its effect is often temporary
- Punishment can cause dysfunctional side effects: workplace conflicts, absenteeism, turnover
- Managers should **ignore** (not punish) undesirable behavior

**Limitation:** Does not account for goals, expectations, or needs. Focuses solely on external consequences.

---

### 3. Designing Motivating Jobs (Job Design)

**Job design** = the way tasks are combined to form complete jobs.

Jobs should be designed deliberately, reflecting: environment demands, organizational technology, employee skills/abilities/preferences.

#### A. Job Enlargement
- **Horizontal** expansion of a job - increasing **job scope** (number of different tasks + frequency of repetition)
- Simply adding more tasks of the same type often fails ("Before I had one lousy job, now I have three")
- **Exception:** Knowledge enlargement (expanding scope of *knowledge* used) -> more job satisfaction, better customer service, fewer errors

#### B. Job Enrichment
- **Vertical** expansion - adding planning and evaluating responsibilities
- Increases **job depth** (degree of control employees have over their work)
- Employees assume tasks formerly done by managers
- Allows greater freedom, independence, responsibility, and self-assessed feedback
- Research evidence on effectiveness is **inconclusive**

#### C. Job Characteristics Model (JCM)

Developed by Hackman & Oldham. Identifies 5 core job dimensions:

| Dimension | Definition |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Skill Variety** | Degree to which job requires use of different skills and talents |
| **Task Identity** | Degree to which job requires completion of a whole, identifiable piece of work |
| **Task Significance** | Degree to which job has substantial impact on lives/work of others |
| **Autonomy** | Degree to which job provides freedom, independence, and discretion in scheduling and procedures |
| **Feedback** | Degree to which carrying out work activities gives direct, clear information about performance effectiveness |

**How they connect:**

| Dimensions | Psychological State | Outcome |
|-----------|---------------------|---------|
| Skill Variety + Task Identity + Task Significance | Experienced meaningfulness of work | High internal work motivation |
| Autonomy | Experienced responsibility for outcomes | High-quality work performance |
| Feedback | Knowledge of actual results | High satisfaction, low absenteeism/turnover |

**Moderator:** Strength of employee **growth need** (desire for self-esteem and self-actualization). High-growth-need individuals respond more positively to enriched jobs. This explains why job enrichment has mixed results - it doesn't work well for low-growth-need individuals.

**JCM Implementation Guidelines:**
1. **Combine tasks** - merge fragmented tasks into larger modules (increases skill variety, task identity)
2. **Create natural work units** - form identifiable, meaningful wholes (increases ownership)
3. **Establish client relationships** - direct worker-client contact (increases skill variety, autonomy, feedback)
4. **Expand jobs vertically** - give employees managerial responsibilities (increases autonomy)
5. **Open feedback channels** - enable direct performance feedback

**Ongoing critiques of JCM:**
- Formulated in the mid-1970s; may not fully capture modern jobs
- Questioned for not capturing social dimensions of work in knowledge/service economies

#### D. Updated Job Design Perspectives

**Relational Perspective:**
- Focuses on the social dimensions of work
- Jobs increasingly depend on interaction and interdependence with coworkers and external parties
- Managers must consider: social support, external interactions, task interdependence, interpersonal feedback

**Proactive Perspective:**
- Employees take initiative to *change* how their work is performed
- Key factors: autonomy, ambiguity/accountability, job complexity, stressors, social context
- Related concept: **high-involvement work practices** - designed to elicit greater input/involvement from workers
- Employee proactivity increases as involvement in decisions increases

---

### 4. Equity Theory

**Core proposition:** Employees compare their input-outcome ratio to that of relevant others. Perceived inequity motivates behavior change.

**Formula:**
```
Your Outcomes / Your Inputs  vs.  Referent's Outcomes / Referent's Inputs
```

**Three states:**
- **Equity** - ratios are equal; no action needed
- **Inequity (underrewarded)** - your ratio < referent's; most impactful on behavior
- **Inequity (overrewarded)** - your ratio > referent's; people rationalize this more easily

**Possible responses to inequity:** lower/higher productivity, improved/reduced output quality, increased absenteeism, voluntary resignation.

**Referent categories:**
- **Persons** - other individuals (coworkers, friends, industry peers)
- **Systems** - organizational pay policies, procedures, allocations
- **Selves** - past personal experiences, prior jobs

**Two types of justice:**

| Type | Definition | Primary Influence |
|------|-----------|------------------|
| **Distributive Justice** | Perceived fairness of *amount and allocation* of rewards | Employee satisfaction |
| **Procedural Justice** | Perceived fairness of the *process* used to determine reward distribution | Organizational commitment, trust in boss, intention to quit |

**Managerial implication:** Even if pay outcomes are perceived as unfair, transparent and consistent procedures can maintain employee trust and commitment.

**Note on equity sensitivity (ES):** Not everyone is equally sensitive to equity. ES is a personality trait based on preferred input-to-outcome ratios. Some individuals tolerate inequity more than others.

---

### 5. Expectancy Theory

Most comprehensive explanation of employee motivation. Supported by most research evidence.

**Core proposition:** An individual acts based on the expectation that the act will be followed by a given outcome AND on the attractiveness of that outcome.

**Three key linkages:**

| Linkage | Variable | Question it Answers |
|---------|----------|-------------------|
| A: Effort -> Performance | Effort-performance linkage | "Can I actually achieve that level of performance if I try?" |
| B: Performance -> Reward | Performance-reward linkage | "Will performing at that level actually get me the reward?" |
| C: Reward -> Individual Goals | Attractiveness | "Do I actually want that reward? Does it align with my goals?" |

**Motivation is maximized only when all three linkages are strong.**

**Key insight:** Reality is irrelevant. What matters is the individual's *perception* of each linkage.

**Managerial implications:**
- Understand what each employee values (not everyone wants the same rewards)
- Make the effort-performance link clear (employees must believe effort leads to performance)
- Make the performance-reward link clear (ensure performance, not seniority or favoritism, drives rewards)
- Align organizational rewards with individual goals
- Clarify expected behaviors and evaluation criteria

---

### 6. Integrating the Contemporary Theories

The theories are complementary, not competing. Key integrations:

- **Goal-setting theory** explains the goals-effort loop: goals direct behavior
- **Expectancy theory** provides the overarching framework: effort -> performance -> rewards -> goals
- **Ability** moderates whether effort converts to performance
- **Objective performance evaluation** is needed for the effort-performance link to be perceived as fair
- **Reinforcement theory** explains why organizational rewards reinforce performance continuation
- **Equity theory** explains how reward comparisons affect effort (inequity = effort change)
- **nAch (three-needs theory)** explains why high achievers bypass the standard effort-performance-reward chain - they are internally driven; the job itself (personal responsibility, feedback, moderate risk) is their reward
- **JCM** improves the effort-performance link by making jobs more intrinsically motivating, and improves goal satisfaction by giving employees more control

## 16.4 Current Issues in Motivation

---

### Managing Cross-Cultural Motivational Challenges

Most motivation theories were developed in the US and reflect American cultural values - particularly individualism and achievement orientation.

**Cross-cultural issues by theory:**

**Maslow's Hierarchy:**
- Hierarchy order varies by culture
- In high uncertainty-avoidance countries (Japan, Greece, Mexico): safety needs are foundational
- In high nurturing countries (Denmark, Sweden, Netherlands, Finland): social needs are foundational
- Group work is more motivating in nurturing-oriented cultures

**Achievement Need (nAch):**
- Presupposes willingness to accept moderate risk (low uncertainty avoidance) AND concern with performance
- Best fits US, Canada, Great Britain
- Less applicable in Chile, Portugal, and other low-achievement-orientation cultures

**Equity Theory:**
- US-style pay practices assume high sensitivity to performance-based equity
- In collectivist cultures (former socialist Eastern European countries): employees expect rewards to reflect individual needs, not just performance; often exhibit entitlement attitudes

**Research on individualism and masculinity (GLOBE framework, 50,000+ employees):**
- Individualist cultures (US, Canada): value individual initiative, freedom, achievement
- Collectivist cultures (Iran, Peru, China): emphasize harmony, belonging, consensus; less interested in individual praise
- Masculine/achievement cultures (Japan, Slovakia): material success focus; push hard, reward top performers with high pay
- Nurturing cultures (Sweden, Netherlands): smaller wage gaps; extensive quality-of-life benefits

**Cross-cultural universals:**
- "Interesting work" is valued nearly everywhere (ranked #1 in Belgium, Britain, Israel, US; top 3 in Japan, Netherlands, Germany)
- Growth, achievement, and responsibility ranked top 3 across US, Canada, Australia, Singapore graduate students
- Respect, work-life balance, type of work done are top motivators across 13 countries
- Herzberg's intrinsic factors appear to have cross-cultural applicability

---

### Motivating Unique Groups of Workers

#### Diverse Workforce
- Men: place higher importance on autonomy
- Women: learning opportunities, flexible hours, good interpersonal relations more important
- Millennials: social relationships, team membership
- Single parents, part-time workers, near-retirement workers: all have different motivational profiles
- **Flexible work arrangements** (compressed workweek, flextime, job sharing, telecommuting) help address diversity of needs
- Telecommuting: job satisfaction initially increases with more telecommuting, but levels off and slightly decreases at high hours - not a universal positive

#### Professionals
- Strong, long-term commitment to their field
- Regularly update knowledge; don't limit themselves to standard work hours
- **Money and promotions are low priority** (they're already well-paid and enjoy their work)
- Motivated by: **job challenge**, **interesting problems**, **the work itself**
- Key concept: **Flow** - state of total absorption in work; loss of sense of time; self-motivating; most applicable to professionals
- Managers maximize flow by designing challenging jobs that require creativity and full skill utilization
- Professionals are less likely to stay in jobs lacking challenge or variety

#### Contingent Workers
- Most are not temporary by choice
- Motivated by: opportunity to become permanent, skills development/training opportunities
- Equity problem: when temps work alongside permanent employees doing the same job for less pay/benefits, performance suffers
- Solution: separate the groups or minimize their interdependence
- **Temporary employees** are less satisfied than permanent workers (fewer benefits, lower pay)
- **Independent contractors** are about as satisfied as permanent workers (their skills are recognized; they are valued for expertise)

---

### Designing Appropriate Rewards Programs

#### 1. Open-Book Management
- Senior executives share **key financial information** with all employees
- Goal: employees think like owners, understand how their decisions affect the bottom line
- Employees must first be taught to read and understand financial statements
- Aligns well with millennial values (honesty, transparency)
- Reported outcome: up to 30% increase in productivity and profitability within the first year

#### 2. Employee Recognition Programs
- Plans to encourage specific behaviors by formally appreciating specific employee contributions
- Range from private thank-yous to formal, publicized programs
- Inexpensive - praise is free
- 89% of organizations have some form of recognition program
- Only 12% of employees say they receive frequent appreciation; only 7% say their company is excellent at showing appreciation
- Surveys consistently identify **recognition** as one of the most powerful workplace motivators

#### 3. Pay-for-Performance Programs
- Compensation plans that **vary with some measure of individual or organizational performance**
- Types: piece-rate pay, merit-based pay, profit-sharing, lump-sum bonuses
- ~94% of employers now have some form of variable-pay plan
- Performance measures: individual productivity, team productivity, departmental output, organizational profit

**Why it works (aligned with expectancy theory):**
- Creates a clear performance-reward linkage
- Focuses employee attention on performance metrics
- Reinforces continued high effort

**Potential pitfalls:**
- Can discourage teamwork (employees compete rather than cooperate)
- Employees may neglect tasks not directly tied to the performance measure
- Not appropriate for all organizational contexts

**Evidence:** Merit and bonus pay are positively associated with future employee performance in most research.

---

## Summary of Theories

| Theory | Core Idea | Empirical Support |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| Maslow's Hierarchy | Five needs in order; satisfy lower before higher | Weak - no empirical support |
| Theory X / Theory Y | Two contrasting human nature assumptions | No confirmatory evidence |
| Herzberg Two-Factor | Motivators (intrinsic) vs. Hygiene (extrinsic) are independent | Mixed; methodology criticized |
| Three-Needs (McClelland) | nAch, nPow, nAff drive work behavior | Moderate; nAch best supported |
| Goal-Setting | Specific, difficult goals -> higher performance | Strong |
| Reinforcement | Behavior is a function of consequences | Strong |
| Job Characteristics (JCM) | 5 core dimensions -> psychological states -> outcomes | Strong; some limitations |
| Equity | Perceived input-outcome ratio fairness | Strong |
| Expectancy | Effort -> Performance -> Reward -> Goals (all perceived) | Strong - most comprehensive |
