## LO18.1 - What Is Controlling, and Why Is It Important?

### What Is Controlling?

**Controlling** - the process of monitoring, comparing, and correcting work performance.

- Managers cannot know whether their units are performing properly until they evaluate what activities have been done and compare actual performance with the desired standard
- Effective controls ensure activities are completed in ways that lead to goal attainment
- The effectiveness of controls is determined by how well they help employees and managers achieve their goals
- Applying controls in organizations is not new - accounting and auditing practices have existed since ancient China and Egypt
- In early 20th century, writings of Frederick Taylor, Henri Fayol, and Max Weber addressed controls in the industrial economy: scientifically training workers, creating lines of authority, imposing formal rules and regulations

### Why Is Controlling Important?

Control is the **final link in the functional chain of management**. Its value is seen in three specific areas:

1. **Planning** - Just stating goals or having employees accept goals does not guarantee the necessary actions to accomplish them. Controlling provides the critical link back to planning.

2. **Empowering employees** - Many managers are reluctant to empower employees because they fear something will go wrong for which they would be held responsible. An effective control system provides information and feedback on employee performance, allowing managers to delegate authority while still maintaining accountability.

3. **Protecting the organization and its assets** - Against threats such as natural disasters, financial scandals, workplace violence, global supply chain disruptions, security breaches, and terrorist attacks. Comprehensive controls and backup plans help assure minimal work disruptions.

## LO18.2 - The Control Process

**Control process** - a three-step process of: (1) measuring actual performance, (2) comparing actual performance against a standard, and (3) taking managerial action to correct deviations or inadequate standards.

> Note: The control process assumes that standards of performance *already exist* - these are the specific objectives against which progress is measured, created during the planning function. Planning must *precede* control.

---

### Step 1: Measuring Actual Performance

#### How We Measure

Five sources of information used by managers:

1. **Personal observation**
   - Provides firsthand, intimate knowledge not filtered through others
   - Permits intensive coverage - minor and major activities can be observed
   - Management-by-walking-around can pick up omissions, facial expressions, tones of voice
   - Drawbacks: subject to perceptual biases; consumes a lot of time; suffers from obtrusiveness (employees may interpret it as mistrust)

2. **Statistical reports**
   - Includes computer outputs, graphs, bar charts, numerical displays
   - Easy to visualize and effective for showing relationships
   - Limitation: provides limited information; reports on only a few key areas and often ignores other important factors

3. **Self-monitoring computers**
   - The computerization of everything (Internet of Things) is reshaping the manager's role in the control process
   - Sensors in smartphones, machinery, robotics, tracking devices collect data and initiate corrective action or alert management
   - Human intervention becomes an option rather than a necessity
   - Concern: how employees respond to technology that can monitor their every movement

4. **Oral reports**
   - Information acquired through conferences, meetings, one-to-one conversations, or telephone calls
   - Fast, allow for feedback, permit language expression and tone of voice
   - Drawback: information is filtered

5. **Written reports**
   - Slower but more formal than oral reports
   - Greater comprehensiveness and conciseness
   - Easy to catalogue and reference

#### What We Measure

- *What* we measure is probably more critical than *how* we measure
- The selection of the wrong criteria can result in serious dysfunctional consequences
- Some control criteria apply to any management situation (e.g., employee satisfaction, turnover rates, absenteeism rates, keeping costs within budget)
- Other criteria should reflect the specific activities that managers supervise
- Most jobs and activities can be expressed in tangible and measurable terms
- When a performance indicator cannot be stated in quantifiable terms, managers should use subjective measures - they are better than no standards at all

---

### Step 2: Comparing Actual Performance Against the Standard

- The comparing step determines the **variation** between actual performance and the standard
- Some variation in performance can be expected in all activities
- Critical to determine an acceptable **range of variation**
- Deviations outside this range need attention
- Both overvariance and undervariance may require managerial attention

**Range of variation** - the acceptable parameters of variance between actual performance and the standard

---

### Step 3: Taking Managerial Action

Managers can choose among three possible courses of action:

1. **Do nothing** - when performance meets the standard within the acceptable range
2. **Correct actual performance**
3. **Revise the standard**

#### Correct Actual Performance

- If the source of variation is deficient performance, corrective action is needed
- Examples of corrective action: changes in strategy, structure, compensation practices, training programs; redesign of jobs; replacement of personnel
- Two types of corrective action:
  - **Immediate corrective action** - corrects problems at once to get performance back on track
  - **Basic corrective action** - asks how and why performance deviated, then corrects the source of the deviation
- Effective managers analyze deviations and take the time to permanently correct significant variances

#### Revise the Standard

- The variance may be a result of an unrealistic standard (too low or too high)
- If performance consistently exceeds the goal, the goal may be too easy and needs to be raised
- Managers must be cautious about revising a standard downward - employees and managers who don't meet the standard will often attack the standard itself first
- If the standard is realistic, hold your ground; reaffirm expectations and take the necessary corrective action

## LO18.3 - Controlling for Organizational and Employee Performance

### What Is Organizational Performance?

**Performance** - the end result of an activity

**Organizational performance** - the accumulated results of all the organization's work activities

### Measures of Organizational Performance

Three commonly used measures:

#### 1. Organizational Productivity

**Productivity** - the amount of goods or services produced divided by the inputs needed to generate that output

- Organizations want to produce the most goods and services using the least amount of inputs
- Output = sales revenue (selling price x number sold)
- Input = costs of acquiring and transforming resources into outputs
- Management's job is to increase the output/input ratio
- Options: raise prices of outputs (often not viable in competitive environments) OR decrease inputs by being more efficient

#### 2. Organizational Effectiveness

**Organizational effectiveness** - a measure of how appropriate organizational goals are and how well those goals are being met

- The bottom line for managers
- Guides managerial decisions in designing strategies, work activities, and coordinating employee work

#### 3. Industry and Company Rankings

- Rankings are a popular way for managers to measure organizational performance
- Rankings are determined by specific performance measures, which differ for each list
- Sources include: Fortune, Forbes, INC., IndustryWeek, Customer Satisfaction Indexes

### Controlling for Employee Performance

#### Delivering Effective Performance Feedback

Managers need to provide employees with feedback so they know where they stand in terms of job performance.

**How to Be a Pro at Giving Feedback:**

1. **Be straightforward by focusing on specific behaviors** - feedback should be specific rather than general; vague statements don't tell the recipient enough to correct behavior or know what to repeat
2. **Be realistic** - focus feedback on what can be changed; commenting on things over which employees have no control is frustrating
3. **Keep feedback impersonal** - feedback, particularly negative feedback, should be descriptive rather than judgmental or evaluative; keep it focused on job-related behaviors
4. **Keep feedback goal oriented** - directed toward the recipient's goals; if the feedback primarily serves to "blow off steam," withhold it
5. **Know when to give feedback - make it well timed** - most meaningful when there is a short interval between behavior and feedback; making it prompt merely for promptness can backfire if you have insufficient information or are emotionally upset
6. **Ensure understanding** - make feedback concise and complete; have the recipient rephrase it to confirm understanding
7. **Watch your body language, tone of voice, and facial expressions** - these can speak louder than words

#### Using Disciplinary Actions

**Disciplinary actions** - actions taken by a manager to enforce the organization's work standards and regulations

Types of discipline problems:
- **Attendance** - absenteeism, tardiness, abuse of sick leave
- **On-the-job behaviors** - insubordination, failure to use safety devices, alcohol or drug abuse
- **Dishonesty** - theft, lying to supervisors, falsifying information on employment applications or other organizational forms
- **Outside activities** - criminal activities, unauthorized strike activities, working for a competing organization (if no-compete clause is part of employment)

**Progressive disciplinary action** - discipline that moves sequentially through steps to ensure that the minimum penalty appropriate to the offense is imposed

Five steps of progressive disciplinary action:
1. Oral warning
2. Initial written warning
3. Final written warning
4. Termination review or suspension
5. Dismissal letter stating the reason for dismissal

- Legal challenges to disciplinary actions have typically favored management when actions were carefully documented and followed the progressive steps

## LO18.4 - Tools for Measuring Organizational Performance

### Types of Control

Three types based on timing:

#### Feedforward Control

**Feedforward control** - control that takes place *before* a work activity is done

- The most desirable type - prevents problems
- Key: taking managerial action *before* a problem occurs
- Problems can be prevented rather than corrected after damage has been done
- Drawback: requires timely and accurate information that is not always easy to get

#### Concurrent Control

**Concurrent control** - control that takes place *while* a work activity is in progress

- The best-known form is **direct supervision**
- All managers can benefit, but especially first-line managers, as they can correct problems before they become too costly

#### Feedback Control

**Feedback control** - control that takes place *after* a work activity is done

- The most popular type of control
- Major problem: by the time a manager has the information, problems have already occurred, leading to waste, damage, and/or additional costs
- In many work areas, it is the only viable type of control
- Two advantages:
  1. Gives managers meaningful information on how effective their planning efforts were
  2. Can enhance motivation - people want to know how well they are doing

---

### Financial Controls

Managers use financial controls to ensure a business earns a profit. Common tools:

#### Financial Ratios

| Objective | Ratio | Calculation | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquidity | Current ratio | Current assets / Current liabilities | Tests ability to meet short-term obligations |
| Liquidity | Acid test | (Current assets - inventories) / Current liabilities | Tests liquidity more accurately when inventories turn over slowly |
| Leverage | Debt to assets | Total debt / Total assets | Higher ratio = more leveraged organization |
| Leverage | Times interest earned | Profits before interest and taxes / Total interest charges | Measures how many times the organization can meet interest expenses |
| Activity | Inventory turnover | Sales / Inventory | Higher ratio = more efficiently inventory assets are used |
| Activity | Total asset turnover | Sales / Total assets | Fewer assets to achieve given sales level = more efficient management |
| Profitability | Profit margin on sales | Net profit after taxes / Total sales | Identifies profits generated |
| Profitability | Return on investment | Net profit after taxes / Total assets | Measures efficiency of assets to generate profits |

#### Budgets

- Budgets are both **planning and control tools**
- As a planning tool: indicates which work activities are important and what resources should be allocated
- As a control tool: provides managers with quantitative standards against which to measure and compare resource consumption
- If deviations are significant, the manager examines what happened and why, then takes necessary action

---

### Information Controls

Managers deal with information controls in two ways:
1. As a tool to help control other organizational activities
2. As an organizational area they need to control

#### How Information Is Used in Controlling

Managers need the right information at the right time and in the right amount to:
- Monitor and measure organizational activities and performance
- Compare actual performance with standards
- Determine if deviations are acceptable
- Develop appropriate courses of action

**Management Information System (MIS)** - a system used to provide management with needed information on a regular basis

- Can be manual or computer-based (almost all organizations today use computer-supported applications)
- The term *system* implies order, arrangement, and purpose
- An MIS focuses on providing managers with *information* (processed and analyzed data), not merely *data* (raw, unanalyzed facts)
- An MIS collects data and turns it into relevant information for managers to use

#### Controlling Information

- Managers must have comprehensive and secure controls in place to protect information
- Controls can range from data encryption to system firewalls to data backups
- Problems can lurk in unexpected places: blogs, search engines, social media
- Equipment such as tablets, laptops, smartphones, and RFID tags are vulnerable to viruses and hacking
- Information controls should be monitored regularly to ensure all possible precautions are in place

---

### Balanced Scorecard

**Balanced scorecard** - a performance measurement tool that looks at performance from more than just the financial perspective

Four areas that contribute to a company's performance:
1. Financial
2. Customer
3. Internal processes
4. People/innovation/growth assets

- Managers should develop goals in each of the four areas and then measure whether the goals are being met
- In practice, managers tend to focus on areas that drive their organization's success
- However, focusing on only one performance area is insufficient - others are affected as well
- The four areas are interrelated and support each other

---

### Benchmarking of Best Practices

**Benchmarking** - identifying, analyzing, and adopting the best practices from other organizations that lead to their superior performance

**Benchmark** - the standard of excellence against which to measure and compare

- Used in healthcare, education, financial services, manufacturing, and other industries
- Primary benefit: allows management to gain an independent perspective on how well their organization performs compared to others
- Best practices are not only found externally - they can also exist inside the organization

**Suggestions for Internal Benchmarking (Exhibit 18-10):**
1. Connect best practices to strategies and goals
2. Identify best practices throughout the organization
3. Develop best practices reward and recognition systems - individuals must be given incentive to share knowledge
4. Communicate best practices throughout the organization
5. Create a best practices knowledge-sharing system - a formal mechanism for organizational members to continue sharing ideas
6. Nurture best practices on an ongoing basis - create a culture that reinforces a "we can learn from everyone" attitude

## LO18.5 - Contemporary Issues in Control

### Social Media as a Control Tool

- Businesses and government agencies use social media to get feedback on what their organizations are doing well and where they are lacking
- Supplements traditional mail and phone surveys
- Provides candid comments and assessments from customers, clients, and citizens

Key statistics:
- 90% of customers provide feedback to companies via social media
- 36% of customers whose feedback is ignored will use social media to shame the brand

---

### Global Differences in Control

- Control techniques can differ significantly across countries
- Differences are primarily in the **measurement** and **corrective action** steps
- In global corporations, managers of foreign operations tend to be less controlled by the home office due to distance
- Distance creates a tendency to formalize controls - organizations often rely on extensive formal reports, communicated electronically

Technology impact:
- Technologically advanced nations: managers use indirect control devices such as computer-generated reports, standardized rules, and direct supervision
- Less technologically advanced countries: managers tend to use more direct supervision and highly centralized decision making

Additional challenges for global managers:
- **Legal constraints** - some countries' laws prohibit closing facilities, laying off employees, taking money out of the country, or bringing in new management from outside the country
- **Comparability** - differences in production methods (e.g., labor-intensive vs. capital-intensive) make cross-facility comparisons difficult
- **Disaster and turmoil preparedness** - global organizations need controls for protecting workers and assets during global turmoil and disasters; feedforward controls are best implemented before an emergency occurs

---

### Workplace Privacy

Employers can legally (and many do) monitor employees in the following ways:
- Read email (even those marked "personal" or "confidential")
- Tap telephone
- Monitor work by computer
- Monitor in employee bathrooms or dressing rooms
- Track whereabouts in a company vehicle

Survey data:
- 66% of US companies monitor internet use
- 45% log keystrokes
- 43% track emails

Reasons managers monitor employees:
1. **Productivity** - employees are hired to work, not for personal internet use; recreational on-the-job web surfing costs billions in lost productivity annually
2. **Legal liability** - to avoid being sued for creating a hostile workplace environment due to offensive messages or inappropriate images
3. **Security** - to ensure company secrets are not being leaked, even inadvertently

- Organizations should have workplace monitoring policies
- Such policies should control employee behavior in a nondemeaning way
- Employees should be informed about those policies

---

### Employee Theft

**Employee theft** - any unauthorized taking of company property by employees for their personal use

- Up to 85% of all organizational theft and fraud is committed by employees, not outsiders
- Estimated cost in the US: $50 billion a year
- Ranges from embezzlement to fraudulent expense reports to removing equipment, parts, software, or office supplies

**Why employees steal (perspectives from different fields):**
- **Industrial security** - opportunity presents itself through lax controls and favorable circumstances
- **Criminologists** - financial-based pressures (personal financial problems) or vice-based pressures (gambling debts)
- **Clinical psychologists** - people rationalize the behavior as correct and appropriate ("everyone does it," "they had it coming," etc.)

**Controlling employee theft using feedforward, concurrent, and feedback controls:**

| Feedforward | Concurrent | Feedback |
|---|---|---|
| Use careful prehiring screening | Treat employees with respect and dignity | Make sure employees know when theft/fraud has occurred (without naming names) |
| Establish specific policies defining theft and fraud and discipline procedures | Openly communicate the costs of stealing | Use the services of professional investigators |
| Involve employees in writing policies | Let employees know regularly about successes in preventing theft and fraud | Redesign control measures |
| Educate and train employees about the policies | Use video surveillance equipment if conditions warrant | Evaluate your organization's culture and manager-employee relationships |
| Have a professional review of internal security controls | Install "lock-out" options on computers, telephones, and email; use corporate hotlines; set a good example | - |

---

### Corporate Governance

**Corporate governance** - the system used to govern a corporation so that the interests of corporate owners are protected

Three areas where reform has taken place:

#### 1. The Role of Boards of Directors

- Original purpose: a group, independent from management, looking out for shareholder interests
- Problem: board members are often selected by management and expected to be loyal and supportive of management's interests
- The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002** puts greater demands on board members of publicly traded companies in the US to do what they were empowered and expected to do
- The **Business Roundtable** has developed a document outlining principles of corporate governance focused on integrity, accountability, and effective engagement between public companies, boards of directors, and investors

#### 2. Financial Reporting and the Audit Committee

- The Sarbanes-Oxley Act called for more disclosure and transparency of corporate financial information
- CEOs and CFOs of public companies in the US are now required to certify their companies' financial results
- Such changes have led to better, more accurate information that is reflective of a company's actual financial condition

#### 3. Compliance Offices and Positions

**Corporate compliance** - the process of making sure a company and its employees follow the laws, regulations, standards, and ethical practices that apply to the organization

- Covers both internal policies and rules and federal and state laws
- **Compliance officer** - the person responsible for ensuring an organization conducts its business in full compliance with all national and internal laws and regulations
- The compliance officer ensures the company conducts business in compliance with all national and internal laws and regulations pertaining to the specific industry, as well as professional standards, accepted business practices, and internal standards
