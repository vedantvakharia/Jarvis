## 17.1 Who Are Leaders, and What Is Leadership?

**Leader** - someone who can influence others and who has managerial authority.
**Leadership** - a process of influencing a group to achieve goals. It is what leaders *do*.

---

## 17.2 Early Leadership Theories

Early theories focused on two angles:
1. The **leader** themselves - trait theories
2. How the **leader interacted** with group members - behavioral theories

---

### Leadership Trait Theories

Research in the 1920s-1930s tried to isolate traits (characteristics) that would differentiate leaders from non-leaders. Traits studied included physical stature, appearance, social class, emotional stability, fluency of speech, and sociability.

**Result:** Proved impossible to identify a universal set of traits that always differentiates a leader from a non-leader.

Later research shifted to identifying traits associated with *leadership as a process* (not just the person), and was more successful.

#### Ten Traits Associated with Effective Leadership

1. **Drive** - high effort level, desire for achievement, ambition, energy, persistence, initiative.
2. **Desire to lead** - strong desire to influence and lead others; willingness to take responsibility.
3. **Honesty and integrity** - truthfulness, high consistency between word and deed.
4. **Self-confidence** - absence of self-doubt; convinces followers of the rightness of goals and decisions.
5. **Intelligence** - ability to gather, synthesize, and interpret large amounts of information; create visions, solve problems, make correct decisions.
6. **Job-relevant knowledge** - deep knowledge of company, industry, and technical matters for well-informed decisions.
7. **Extraversion** - energetic, lively, sociable, assertive.
8. **Proneness to guilt** - positively related to effectiveness; produces strong sense of responsibility for others.
9. **Emotional intelligence** - sensing others' needs, listening to followers (including what they don't say), reading reactions.
10. **Conscientiousness** - disciplined, able to keep commitments.

**Key limitation of trait theories:** Traits alone are not sufficient. They ignore interactions between leaders and group members, and ignore situational factors. Traits only make it *more likely* that someone will be effective - they don't guarantee it.

---

### Leadership Behavioral Theories
#### 1. University of Iowa Studies

Explored three leadership styles:

- **Autocratic style** - leader dictates work methods, makes unilateral decisions, limits employee participation.
- **Democratic style** - leader involves employees in decision making, delegates authority, uses feedback for coaching.
- **Laissez-faire style** - leader lets the group make decisions and complete work however they see fit.

**Findings:**
- Democratic style initially seemed to produce both good quantity and quality of work.
- Later studies showed **mixed results** for performance.
- More consistent finding: group members were **more satisfied** under a democratic leader than an autocratic one.
- Created a dilemma: should a leader focus on higher performance or higher member satisfaction?

---

#### 2. Ohio State Studies

Identified two key dimensions of leader behavior from 1,000+ behavioral dimensions:

- **Initiating structure** - the extent to which a leader defines their own role and the roles of group members in attaining goals. Includes organizing work, work relationships, and goals.
- **Consideration** - the extent to which a leader has work relationships characterized by mutual trust and respect for group members' ideas and feelings. Includes helping with personal problems, being friendly and approachable, treating members as equals.

**Key concept:** A **high-high leader** is high in both initiating structure and consideration.

**Finding:** A high-high leader sometimes achieved high subordinate performance and satisfaction - but NOT in all situations.

---

#### 3. University of Michigan Studies

Conducted around the same time as Ohio State. Also identified two dimensions:

- **Employee-oriented leaders** - emphasized interpersonal relationships and caring for employees' needs.
- **Production-oriented leaders** - emphasized technical or task aspects of the job.

**Finding:** Employee-oriented leaders were associated with **high group productivity AND higher job satisfaction** - a cleaner conclusion than Ohio State.

---

#### 4. The Managerial Grid

Built on the behavioral dimensions from the above studies. Created a two-dimensional grid:

- **Concern for people** (vertical axis) - rated 1 (low) to 9 (high)
- **Concern for production** (horizontal axis) - rated 1 (low) to 9 (high)

Five named styles on the grid:
- **(1,1) Impoverished management** - low concern for both production and people.
- **(9,1) Task management** - high concern for production, low concern for people.
- **(5,5) Middle-of-the-road management** - medium concern for both.
- **(1,9) Country club management** - low concern for production, high concern for people.
- **(9,9) Team management** - high concern for both production and people.

**Finding:** Researchers concluded managers performed best using a 9,9 style. However, there is **little substantive evidence** to support that 9,9 is most effective in all situations. The grid only provided a framework for conceptualizing style, not definitive answers.

---

#### Limitation of All Behavioral Theories

Behavioral research revealed the dual nature of leadership (task focus + people focus) but still could not identify a universally effective style. This prompted a move toward examining **situational factors**.

---

## 17.3 Contingency Theories of Leadership

The corporate world is full of leaders who failed because they misunderstood the context they were in. Contingency theories examine the *if-then* relationship: *if* this is the situation, *then* this is the best leadership style.

Three major contingency theories: **Fiedler, Hersey-Blanchard (SLT), and Path-Goal**.

---

### The Fiedler Contingency Model

Developed by Fred Fiedler. Proposes that effective group performance depends on properly matching the leader's style with the degree of control and influence the situation allows.

**Core premise:** A certain leadership style will be most effective in different types of situations.

#### Step 1: Identify Leadership Style - The LPC Questionnaire

**Least-Preferred Coworker (LPC) questionnaire** - 18 pairs of contrasting adjectives (e.g., pleasant-unpleasant, cold-warm). Respondents rate the one person they *least* enjoyed working with on a scale of 1-8 for each pair.

- **High LPC score** (described least-preferred coworker positively) = **relationship-oriented** leader - primarily interested in good personal relations.
- **Low LPC score** (described least-preferred coworker negatively) = **task-oriented** leader - primarily interested in productivity and getting the job done.

**Critical assumption:** Fiedler assumed a person's leadership style is **fixed and stable**, regardless of the situation. You cannot change it.

#### Step 2: Define the Situation - Three Contingency Dimensions

1. **Leader-member relations** - degree of confidence, trust, and respect employees have for their leader. Rated: *good or poor*.
2. **Task structure** - degree to which job assignments are formalized and structured. Rated: *high or low*.
3. **Position power** - degree of influence a leader has over activities such as hiring, firing, discipline, promotions, and salary increases. Rated: *strong or weak*.

Combining these three produces **eight possible situational categories (I-VIII)**:
- **Categories I, II, III** - highly favorable for the leader.
- **Categories IV, V, VI** - moderately favorable for the leader.
- **Categories VII, VIII** - highly unfavorable for the leader.

#### Fiedler's Conclusions (from studying 1,200 groups)

- **Task-oriented leaders** perform better in **very favorable** AND **very unfavorable** situations.
- **Relationship-oriented leaders** perform better in **moderately favorable** situations.

#### Improving Leader Effectiveness (since style is fixed)

Only two options:
1. **Replace the leader** with someone whose style fits the situation.
2. **Change the situation** to fit the leader - by restructuring tasks, increasing/decreasing the leader's position power, or improving leader-member relations.

#### Criticisms of Fiedler's Model

- Unrealistic to assume a person *cannot* change their leadership style. Effective leaders do change styles.
- The LPC questionnaire is not very practical.
- Situational variables are difficult to assess.
- Despite these criticisms, the model showed that effective leadership must reflect situational factors.

---

### Hersey and Blanchard's Situational Leadership Theory (SLT)

A contingency theory focused on **followers' readiness**.

**Why focus on followers?** Because it is the followers who ultimately accept or reject the leader. Group effectiveness depends on follower actions.

**Readiness** - the extent to which people have the **ability** and **willingness** to accomplish a specific task.

#### Four Leadership Styles (combining task and relationship behaviors)

1. **Telling** (high task - low relationship) - leader defines roles and tells people what, how, when, and where to do tasks.
2. **Selling** (high task - high relationship) - leader provides both directive and supportive behavior.
3. **Participating** (low task - high relationship) - leader and followers share in decision making; leader facilitates and communicates.
4. **Delegating** (low task - low relationship) - leader provides little direction or support.

#### Four Stages of Follower Readiness

- **R1** - unable AND unwilling. Followers are not competent or confident.
- **R2** - unable but willing. Followers are motivated but lack appropriate skills.
- **R3** - able but unwilling. Followers are competent but don't want to do something.
- **R4** - able AND willing. Followers can and will do what is asked.

#### Matching Style to Readiness

| Readiness | Style |
|-----------|-------|
| R1 (unable, unwilling) | Telling |
| R2 (unable, willing) | Selling |
| R3 (able, unwilling) | Participating |
| R4 (able, willing) | Delegating |

The SLT views the leader-follower relationship like a parent-child relationship: as the follower matures (readiness increases), the leader decreases both task control and relationship behaviors.

#### Evaluation of SLT

- Has intuitive appeal and acknowledges the importance of followers.
- Research results have been **generally disappointing**.
- Problems include internal inconsistencies in the model and research methodology issues.
- Despite wide popularity, should not be enthusiastically endorsed without caution.

---

### Path-Goal Theory (Robert House)

**Core idea:** The leader's job is to provide followers with the information, support, or other resources necessary to achieve their goals. Effective leaders remove roadblocks and pitfalls to give followers a clearer path to goal achievement.

Derived from **expectancy theory of motivation**.

**Key difference from Fiedler:** House assumed leaders are **flexible** and can display any or all leadership styles depending on the situation.

#### Four Leadership Behaviors

1. **Directive leader** - tells subordinates what is expected, schedules work, gives specific guidance on how to accomplish tasks.
2. **Supportive leader** - shows concern for followers' needs; is friendly.
3. **Participative leader** - consults with group members and uses their suggestions before deciding.
4. **Achievement-oriented leader** - sets challenging goals and expects followers to perform at their highest level.

#### Two Sets of Contingency Variables (Exhibit 17-4)

**Environmental contingency factors** (outside follower's control):
- Task structure
- Formal authority system
- Work group

**Subordinate contingency factors** (personal characteristics):
- Locus of control
- Experience
- Perceived ability

A leader's behavior will NOT be effective if it is redundant with what the environment already provides, or if it is incongruent with follower characteristics.

#### Specific Predictions from Path-Goal Theory

- **Directive** leadership -> greater satisfaction when tasks are **ambiguous or stressful** (followers need direction).
- **Supportive** leadership -> high performance and satisfaction when tasks are **highly structured** (leader only needs to support, not direct).
- **Directive** leadership is seen as **redundant** when subordinates have **high ability or experience** (they don't need to be told what to do).
- The **clearer and more bureaucratic** the formal authority, the more leaders should use **supportive** behavior and de-emphasize directive behavior.
- **Directive** leadership -> higher satisfaction when there is **substantive conflict** within the work group (followers need someone to take charge).
- **Internal locus of control** -> more satisfied with **participative** style (they believe they control their outcomes).
- **External locus of control** -> more satisfied with **directive** style (they believe outcomes are externally determined).
- **Achievement-oriented** leadership -> increases subordinates' expectancy that effort leads to high performance when tasks are **ambiguously structured**.

#### Evaluation of Path-Goal Theory

- Research reviews suggest **mixed support**.
- Overall summary: employee performance and satisfaction are positively influenced when the leader compensates for shortcomings in either the employee or the work setting.
- If the leader over-explains things that are already clear, or directs highly capable employees, they are likely to be seen as redundant or insulting.

## 17.4 Contemporary Views of Leadership

---

### Leader-Member Exchange (LMX) Theory

Leaders create **in-groups** and **out-groups** among their followers.

- **In-group** members - higher performance ratings, less turnover, greater job satisfaction.
- **Out-group** members - lower standing with the leader, less favorable outcomes.

Early in the relationship, a leader implicitly categorizes a follower as an "in" or "out." This tends to remain stable over time.

In-group members tend to share demographic, attitude, personality, and even gender similarities with the leader, OR have higher competence than out-group members.

**Research support:** LMX research has been generally supportive. Leaders do differentiate among followers non-randomly. In-group members engage in more helping/"citizenship" behaviors and report greater satisfaction with their boss.

---

### Charismatic Leadership

**Charismatic leader** - someone to whom followers attribute heroic or extraordinary leadership abilities when they observe certain behaviors and tend to give power.

#### Five Key Characteristics of Charismatic Leaders

1. They have a **vision**.
2. They have the ability to **articulate that vision**.
3. They are **willing to take risks** to achieve the vision.
4. They are **sensitive** to both environmental constraints and follower needs.
5. They exhibit **behaviors that are out of the ordinary**.

#### Charismatic Leadership and Performance

- Growing evidence shows impressive correlations between charismatic leadership and high performance and satisfaction among followers.
- One study found charismatic CEOs had **no impact** on subsequent organizational performance - charisma's value is debated.
- Charisma has a **potential downside**: narcissistic and corrupt charismatic leaders can allow personal goals to override organizational goals (e.g., Enron, Tyco, WorldCom, HealthSouth).

#### Can Charisma Be Learned?

Most experts believe charismatic behaviors **can be trained**. Training focuses on:
- Articulating a far-reaching goal.
- Communicating high performance expectations.
- Exhibiting confidence in subordinates' ability.
- Empathizing with subordinates' needs.
- Projecting a powerful, confident, dynamic presence.
- Using a captivating and engaging voice tone.
- Charismatic nonverbal behaviors: leaning toward the follower, maintaining direct eye contact, relaxed posture, animated facial expressions.

**When is charisma most useful?**
Charismatic leadership may not always be necessary. It is most appropriate when:
- The follower's task has an ideological purpose.
- The environment involves high degrees of stress and uncertainty.
- This explains why charismatic leaders surface more often in politics, religion, wartime, business startups, or organizational survival crises.

---

### Transformational-Transactional Leadership

#### Transactional Leaders
- Lead primarily by using **social exchanges (transactions)**.
- Guide or motivate followers toward established goals by **exchanging rewards for productivity**.

#### Transformational Leaders
- **Stimulate and inspire (transform)** followers to achieve extraordinary outcomes.
- Pay attention to the concerns and developmental needs of followers.
- Change followers' awareness of issues by helping them look at old problems in new ways.
- Excite, arouse, and inspire followers to exert extra effort toward group goals.

#### Relationship Between the Two

- These are **NOT opposing approaches** - transformational leadership **develops from** transactional leadership.
- Transformational leadership produces effort and performance levels **beyond** what a transactional approach alone achieves.
- Transformational leadership is **more than charisma**: it also instills in followers the ability to question not only established views but also those views held by the leader.

#### Evidence for Transformational Leadership

Overwhelmingly impressive support:
- Transformational leaders are evaluated as more effective, higher performers, more promotable, and more interpersonally sensitive.
- Strongly correlated with lower turnover rates and higher levels of: productivity, employee satisfaction, creativity, goal attainment, follower well-being, and corporate entrepreneurship (especially in start-ups).

---

### Authentic Leadership

**Authentic leaders** know who they are, know what they believe in, and act on those values and beliefs openly and candidly.

#### Four Qualities of Authentic Leaders

1. **Self-aware** - they know themselves.
2. **Transparent** - they are open about themselves.
3. **Openly solicit views** that challenge their deeply held positions.
4. **Guided by strong moral standards**.
5. They are also typically **humble**.

When leaders practice what they preach (act on their values openly), followers tend to develop a **strong affective commitment and trust** in the leader, and to a lesser degree improve their performance and organizational citizenship behavior.

---

### Ethical Leadership

Leaders set ethical expectations for all members. Ethical top leadership influences not only direct followers but spreads down the entire command structure.

**Effects of highly ethical leaders:**
- Evaluated very positively by subordinates.
- Subordinates are more satisfied and committed to their jobs.
- Subordinates experience less strain and lower turnover intentions.
- Subordinates are more motivated, perform better, engage in more organizational citizenship behaviors, and fewer counterproductive work behaviors.

**Key principle:** Leadership is NOT value-free. Effectiveness must account for both the *means* a leader uses AND the *content* of the goals.

---

### Servant Leadership

An extension of ethical leadership.

**Servant leaders** go beyond self-interest and focus on **opportunities to help followers grow and develop**. They put the needs of followers ahead of their own needs.

#### Specific Characteristics of Servant Leadership

- Caring about followers' personal problems and well-being.
- Empowering followers with responsibility and decision-making influence.
- Helping subordinates grow and succeed.
- Serving as a model of integrity.

#### Research Evidence

- A study of 71 restaurant general managers found servant leaders tend to create a **culture of service**, which improves restaurant performance and enhances employee attitudes.
- Servant leadership predicted **customer satisfaction** and service performance in a study of Chinese hairstylists.

#### Cultural Context

- Servant leadership may be more prevalent and effective in **certain cultures** (e.g., East Asian cultures prototype leadership as servant-like - the leader at the back of the group, unifying rather than commanding).

---

### Followership

A leader cannot exist without followers. Leaders can only be as effective as their ability to engage followers. Followers influence the attitudes, aptitudes, and behaviors of leaders - the relationship is **two-way**.

For success, followers need a set of skills that are **complementary** to those of the leader.

#### Five Characteristics of Effective Followers (today's standard)

1. **Can manage themselves** - self-directed, self-motivated, can work without close supervision.
2. **Strongly committed to a purpose outside themselves** - committed to a cause, product, team, or organization beyond just themselves.
3. **Enthusiastic** - positive attitude, "buy into" the leader's vision.
4. **Build competence and focus efforts for maximum impact** - master skills useful to the organization, hold higher performance standards than required.
5. **Courageous, honest, and credible** - independent, critical thinkers; hold high ethical standards; give credit where it's due; admit mistakes.

---

## 17.5 Integrating Theories of Leadership

---

### Traits

- No single trait or set of traits is unique to leaders.
- Several traits **regularly appear** in research: most powerful are **intelligence, emotional intelligence, and conscientiousness**.
- These traits may more accurately reflect the *perception* of leadership rather than actual leadership effectiveness.
- Conclusion: even if traits play a role, it is **small and dependent on situational factors**.

---

### Behaviors

All the behavioral dimensions studied (democratic/autocratic/laissez-faire; initiating structure/consideration; employee-oriented/production-oriented; concern for people/concern for production) show **considerable overlap, if not redundancy**.

Transitional, transformational, charismatic, and LMX theories also all include leadership behaviors.

Efforts to streamline produced **three metacategories**:

1. **Task-oriented behavior** - transactional leadership, initiating structure, directive behavior, production orientation.
2. **Relations-oriented behavior** - consideration, democratic style, employee/people oriented, participative behavior, transformational leadership, LMX in-group treatment.
3. **Change-oriented behavior** - communicating a vision of change, encouraging innovative thinking, risk-taking; a major component of both transformational and charismatic leadership.

**Finding:** Relations-oriented leadership accounted for **most of the differences** in both employee commitment and job performance.

**Overlap of ethical/authentic and transformational leadership:** Transformational leadership appears to **encompass almost all the same variables** as ethical and authentic leadership. These concepts don't offer much beyond what transformational leadership already provides. Transformational leadership is related to employee commitment, trust, satisfaction, and performance.

---

### Contingency Factors

Four stand out as particularly relevant:

1. **Follower experience** - more experienced employees are less dependent on the leader. A leader's guidance is especially important for new employees. Experienced employees may find task-oriented leader behavior unnecessary or even insulting.
2. **Follower ability** - high-ability employees can perform with minimal supervision, similar to experienced employees.
3. **Organizational culture** - shapes what leadership style is appropriate. What is "appropriate" in a prison or military organization differs greatly from a consulting firm or university.
4. **National culture** - shapes what is acceptable. The same relations-oriented style appropriate and effective in Sweden may be seen as weak and ineffective in Mexico.

---

## 17.6 Leadership Issues in the Twenty-First Century

---

### Managing Power

Leaders get their power - the right and capacity to influence work actions or decisions - from **five sources**:

1. **Legitimate power** - power from position in the organization. Legitimate power = authority.
2. **Coercive power** - power to punish or control. Followers react out of fear of negative results. Examples: suspending employees, assigning undesirable work.
3. **Reward power** - power to give positive rewards. Can be money, favorable performance appraisals, promotions, interesting work assignments, preferred shifts, etc.
4. **Expert power** - power based on expertise, special skills, or knowledge. Enhanced when those skills are critical to the work group.
5. **Referent power** - power arising from a person's desirable resources or personal traits; develops from admiration and the desire to be like that person. Explains why celebrities can influence purchasing decisions.

Most effective leaders rely on **several different forms of power** simultaneously to affect behavior and performance.

---

### Developing Credibility and Trust

**Credibility** - the degree to which followers perceive someone as honest, competent, and able to inspire.

Followers judge a leader's credibility in terms of: **honesty, competence, and ability to inspire**.

**Trust** - the belief in the integrity, character, and ability of a leader. Followers who trust a leader are willing to be vulnerable because they are confident their rights and interests will not be abused.

#### Five Dimensions of Trust

1. **Integrity** - honesty and truthfulness.
2. **Competence** - technical and interpersonal knowledge and skills.
3. **Consistency** - reliability, predictability, and good judgment.
4. **Loyalty** - willingness to protect a person, physically and emotionally.
5. **Openness** - willingness to share ideas and information freely.

**Most critical dimension: integrity.**

#### Why Trust Matters More Than Ever

- Trends toward empowerment and self-managed work teams have **reduced traditional control mechanisms**.
- Leaders increasingly lead people who are physically separated (virtual teams, cross-functional teams, strategic alliance partners). These roles don't allow falling back on formal position for influence.
- Many of these relationships are **fluid and fleeting** - ability to quickly develop and sustain trust is crucial.
- Research: trust in leadership is significantly related to **positive job outcomes** - job performance, organizational citizenship behavior, job satisfaction, organizational commitment.
- Downsizing, financial challenges, and increased use of temporary employees have **undermined employee trust** in leaders. Today's leaders face the challenge of rebuilding and restoring trust.

#### Seven Actions to Build Trust

1. **Be open** - keep people informed, make decision criteria clear, explain rationale, be candid about problems.
2. **Be fair** - consider how decisions will be perceived in terms of objectivity and fairness; be objective in performance appraisals; attend to equity in reward distributions.
3. **Speak your feelings** - leaders who convey only hard facts come across as cold and distant; sharing feelings makes you appear real and human.
4. **Tell the truth** - once you lie and are found out, your ability to gain and hold trust is largely diminished.
5. **Show consistency** - let central values and beliefs guide your actions; people want predictability.
6. **Fulfill your promises** - keep your word and commitments; trust requires people to believe you are dependable.
7. **Maintain confidences** - people trust those who are discreet; if perceived as someone who leaks personal confidences, you won't be seen as trustworthy.

---

### Leading Virtual Teams

**Challenge:** How do you lead people who are physically separated, potentially in different time zones, geographic locations, or cultures?

43% of employed Americans (Gallup) spent at least some time working remotely (at time of publication).

#### What Is Missing in Virtual Interactions?

- Lack of physical contact.
- Difficult to achieve work group cohesion.
- No opportunity for direct supervision.
- Often a time difference.
- **Central issue: communication.**

#### Challenges of Virtual Communication

- Harsh words cannot be softened by nonverbal cues (smiles, body language). This is absent in email/phone.
- Email/text exchanges can escalate conflicts that would never occur face-to-face.
- Online leaders must ensure:
  - The **tone** of their message correctly reflects intended emotions.
  - The **formality level** is appropriate.
  - They do NOT use the same communication style with bosses as with staff.
  - They do NOT use digital communication to "hide" when delivering bad news.

#### Informal Interaction in Virtual Settings

- "Water cooler talk" is absent or restricted. On-site leaders bond, express personality, and build trust through these informal settings.
- Successful virtual leaders substitute with **social media** (Facebook and similar platforms) to share interests, experiences, and work-related content.

#### Virtual Leadership and Skill Sets

- The digital age can turn non-leaders into leaders: those with weak face-to-face skills may shine online if they have strong writing skills.
- Virtual environment works **against** people with strong verbal skills and **for** those with writing skills.

#### Virtual Interpersonal Skills

Different from face-to-face interpersonal skills. Include:
- Communicating support and leadership through written words.
- Reading emotions in others' written messages.
- Developing good phone skills.
- Good writing skills are rapidly becoming an **extension of interpersonal skills** for virtual leaders.

---

### Leadership Training

**Scale of investment:** US companies spend more than $87 billion per year on formal training and development.

**Is it effective?** Individual studies showed mixed results. However, a comprehensive review of 300+ studies found very encouraging results when certain conditions are met.

**Effective leadership training:**
- Composed of **multiple sessions**.
- Combines **information, demonstration, and practice-methods**.
- **Contextualization** - learning is set in the strategy and culture of the organization.
- **Personalization** - participants can seek learning related to their own aspirations.

**Not everyone can be trained to be a leader.** Some people do not have what it takes. Leadership training is more likely to succeed with:
- **High self-monitors** - people with the flexibility to change behavior as situations require.
- People with higher levels of **"motivation to lead."**

**What CAN be taught:**
- Vision-creation may be too optimistic to teach.
- **Implementation skills** can be taught.
- Understanding content themes critical to effective visions.
- Trust-building and mentoring skills.
- **Situational analysis skills** - how to evaluate situations, modify situations to fit style, and assess which leader behaviors are most effective.

---

### When Leadership May Not Be Important

Despite the belief that some leadership style will always be effective, leadership may NOT always matter. Data from numerous studies show that in many situations, whatever actions leaders exhibit are **irrelevant**.

Certain individual, job, and organizational variables can act as:
- **Substitutes for leadership** - make a leader's influence unnecessary (they replace it).
- **Neutralizers of leadership** - make it impossible for leader behavior to make any difference (they negate it).

#### Examples of Substitutes/Neutralizers

- **Employee characteristics:** experience, training, "professional" orientation, or indifference toward organizational rewards.
  - Experience and training can replace the need for a leader's support or ability to create structure and reduce task ambiguity.
- **Job characteristics:** jobs that are inherently unambiguous, routine, or intrinsically satisfying place fewer demands on the leadership variable.
- **Organizational characteristics:** explicit formalized goals, rigid rules and procedures, cohesive work groups can all replace formal leadership.
