## 1. Cognition and Metacognition

### Definitions

- **Cognition:** The mental activities associated with thought, decision making, language, and other higher mental processes. A general term denoting thinking and many other aspects of higher mental processes.
- **Thinking:** An activity that involves the manipulation of mental representations of various features of the external world. Includes reasoning - mental activity through which we transform available information in order to reach conclusions.

---

### Basic Elements of Thought: Concepts, Propositions, Images

Our thoughts consist largely of three basic components.

#### A. Concepts: Categories for Understanding Experience

- **Definition:** Mental categories for objects, events, experiences, or ideas that are similar to one another in one or more respects.
- **Function:** Allow us to represent a great deal of information about diverse objects, events, or ideas in a highly efficient manner. They simplify the complex world of experience.

##### Logical vs. Natural Concepts

| Feature | Logical Concepts | Natural Concepts |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Clearly defined by a set of rules or properties | Not based on a precise set of attributes; no clear-cut boundaries |
| **Membership** | Unambiguous - an object either is or is not a member | Fuzzy around the edges |
| **Example** | A triangle (3 sides, angles sum to 180 degrees); a square (4 equal sides, 4 x 90-degree angles) | "Fruit" (is a tomato a fruit?), "Art" |
| **Basis** | Fixed defining features | Often defined by **prototypes** |
| **World accuracy** | Precise, but rare in natural world | More accurately reflect the natural world |

##### Prototypes

- **Definition:** The best or clearest examples of a natural concept (introduced by **Rosch**, 1975).
- Prototypes emerge from our experience with the external world.
- New items are compared against the prototype; the more attributes they share with it, the more likely they are to be included in the concept.
- **Example:** For "fruit," most people think of apples, peaches, or pears - not avocados or olives. For "art," most think of paintings, drawings, sculptures - not a light show.

#### How Concepts Are Represented

Three proposed mechanisms:

1. **Feature/Attribute Matching:** Attributes associated with a concept are stored in memory; new items are evaluated by comparing their attributes against stored ones. Closer match = more likely inclusion.
2. **Visual Images:** Mental pictures of objects or events in the external world. E.g., imagining two chess players bent over a board when asked if chess is a sport.
3. **Schemas:** Cognitive frameworks that represent our knowledge of and assumptions about the world.

##### Schemas vs. Concepts

| Feature | Concepts | Schemas |
|---|---|---|
| **Complexity** | Simpler | More complex |
| **Scope** | Represent a category | Contain a broad range of information; may include many distinct concepts |
| **Basis** | Acquired through experience | Acquired through experience |
| **Example** | "Dog" | Self-schema (a mental framework holding information about one's own traits, characteristics, and expectations) |

> **Exam Tip:** Key distinction: schemas contain concepts. A schema is the larger structure; a concept is a building block within it.

---

#### B. Propositions: Relations Between Concepts

- **Definition:** Sentences that relate one concept to another and can stand as separate assertions.
- **Examples:**
  - "Ramesh greeted Salman" - describes a relationship between two concepts.
  - "Polar bears have white fur" - describes the relationship between a concept and its properties.
- Clusters of propositions are represented as **Mental Models** - knowledge structures that guide our interactions with objects and events in the world.
- **Danger:** If a mental model is incorrect, or if information within it is processed incompletely, errors result. E.g., an incorrect mental model of how interest rates affect stocks and bonds may cause financial loss.

---

#### C. Images: Mental Pictures of the World

- **Visual Images:** Mental pictures or representations of objects or events.
- Mental manipulations performed on images of objects are very similar to those performed on actual objects (**Kosslyn**, 1994).
  - Example: Locating animals in a mental image of a national park takes longer than locating snow because animals require more detailed scanning.
- Once formed, a mental image is perceived and thought about as if it actually existed.
  - Imagining walking toward an object: the image gradually expands, filling the imaginary visual field.
  - Estimating distances in a familiar place: the farther apart the locations, the longer it takes.
- **Practical benefits of mental imagery:**
  - Understanding verbal instructions (converting words into mental pictures of actions).
  - Enhancing moods (visualizing positive events).
  - Helping change behavior to achieve goals (losing weight, enhancing performance).
- **Key finding:** Greatest benefits come from mentally simulating the **process or steps** to reach a goal, not from visualizing the desired **outcome** itself. Visualizing the process forces identification and organization of specific activities and helps avoid the **planning fallacy**.
- **Planning Fallacy:** Our tendency to underestimate the resources (time, money) necessary to achieve a goal and to overestimate how easily the goal can be achieved (**Buehler, Griffin, & Ross**, 1994).

> **Exam Tip:** Very likely exam question - "Why is visualizing the process more beneficial than visualizing the outcome?" Answer: It forces you to identify specific steps, and it combats the planning fallacy.

---

### Metacognitive Processing

- **Definition:** An expanded level of awareness that allows us, in a sense, to observe ourselves engaged in the problem-solving process.
- Talking through a problem diverts attention away from irrelevant aspects and toward aspects important in the search for a solution.

#### Case Study: Berardi-Coletta et al. (1995) - Playing Card Study

> **Setup:** Participants practiced solving progressively more difficult versions of a playing-card ordering problem, then were tested on the hardest version.
>
> **Three groups:**
> 1. **Metacognitive group:** Asked process-oriented questions ("How are you deciding on a way to work out the order?")
> 2. **Problem-focused group:** Asked problem-focused questions ("What is the goal of the problem?")
> 3. **Control group:** No additional instructions.
>
> **Result:** The metacognitive group performed **best** (fewest trials to solve). The problem-focused group performed **worst** (most trials). Conclusion: Talking through a problem is useful especially when it leads to metacognitive processing and a focus on the problem-solving *process*.

## 2. Reasoning

- **Reasoning:** Cognitive activity in which we transform information in order to reach specific conclusions. Drawing conclusions from available information.

### Formal vs. Everyday Reasoning

| Feature | Formal Reasoning | Everyday Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Information** | All required information is supplied | Must work with incomplete information |
| **Problem** | Straightforward | Complex, multifaceted |
| **Answers** | Typically one correct answer | Far less definite |
| **Method** | Follows a specific method | Planning, making commitments, evaluating arguments |
| **Frequency** | Less common | The kind we usually perform |

> **Exam Tip:** Everyday reasoning is not inferior to formal reasoning - it is simply more complex and more representative of real cognitive activity.

---

### Sources of Error in Reasoning

#### 1. The Role of Mood States

- Both negative AND positive moods can reduce ability to reason effectively.
- **Study (Oaksford et al., 1996):** Brief film clips induced positive, negative, or neutral moods; participants then solved difficult reasoning tasks.
  - The **positive mood group** was **less effective** than the other groups.
  - **Why:** Being in a positive mood makes more, and more *diffuse*, memories available. This is an asset for tasks requiring creative solutions. But step-by-step logic relies less on long-term memory retrieval and more on working through discrete steps.
- **Conclusion:** A positive mood state does not guarantee enhanced reasoning ability.

#### 2. The Role of Beliefs

- Reasoning is often influenced by emotion-laden beliefs.
- When powerful beliefs confront logical arguments, it is often the **logical argument** that gives way.
- Mechanism: The listener may alter the meaning of the speaker's premises, or "remember" things never actually said, in order to weaken a conclusion that conflicts with their beliefs.
- **General principle:** Beliefs can override valid logical arguments regardless of the specific issue.

#### 3. Confirmation Bias

- **Definition:** Our strong tendency to test conclusions or hypotheses by examining only, or primarily, evidence that **confirms** our initial views (**Nickerson**, 1998).
- Effect: Individuals become firmly locked into flawed conclusions because they never consider disconfirming information.
- **Mechanism (Figure 7.3):**
  - Evidence **consistent** with present views: Noticed, encoded, processed, remembered -> Present views maintained and strengthened.
  - Evidence **not consistent** with present views: Not noticed, encoded, processed, or remembered.
- **Example:** A person who opposes the death penalty reads only anti-death-penalty articles, or reads pro-death-penalty articles less carefully.

> **Exam Tip:** Confirmation bias operates even before conscious reasoning - it determines what information gets encoded in the first place.

#### 4. Hindsight Effect ("I Knew It All Along")

- **Definition:** The tendency to assume that we would have been better at predicting actual events than is really true; after an event occurs, we believe we could have predicted it (**Hawkins & Hastie**, 1990).
- In many studies, knowing that an event occurred causes individuals to assume they could have predicted it more accurately than is actually the case (**Christensen-Szalanski & Willham**, 1991).

**Self-Serving Dimension of Hindsight:**
- Hindsight is amplified when outcomes reflect positively on the decision maker: "I knew it would be favorable because I made a wise decision."
- Hindsight is reduced or eliminated when outcomes are negative: "The outcome was not foreseeable; therefore, it was not my fault."
- **Louie (1999):** Only participants who received *favorable* decision outcome information exhibited hindsight bias.

> **Case Study: Mount Everest (Krakauer, 1997)**
>
> An experienced climbing guide led a group up Everest. Bad weather contributed to deaths of several hikers, including the guide. The guide may have taken unnecessary risks partly due to hindsight bias: his previous successful climbs may have appeared more inevitable in hindsight than they were in foresight, causing him to overestimate his abilities and ignore critical factors (such as the fact that all previous climbs had occurred in good weather). Focusing on actual outcomes (success) rather than the many ways expeditions could have failed led to a fatal miscalculation.

---

### Physical Reasoning

- **Physical Reasoning:** The way in which people perceive physical objects and the cognitive processes that allow people to make predictions about events involving these objects (**Pani**, 1997).
- Ability to reason about physical objects depends on the fit between perception and knowledge of fundamental properties of physical systems.
- Most important property: **Orientation** - how physical objects are aligned in 3D space.

**Study (Pani, Zhou, & Friend, 1997):** Participants viewed 3D computer objects (cubes, octahedra, tetrahedra) oriented either vertically or nonvertically, then physically represented them on a styrofoam sphere.

| Orientation | Perception | Performance |
|---|---|---|
| Vertical | Symmetrical edges/surfaces; subjectively **simple** | Less time to study; more accurate representation |
| Nonvertical | Nonuniform edges/surfaces; subjectively **complex** | More time to study; less accurate |

## 3. Decision Making

- **Decision Making:** The process of choosing among various courses of action or alternatives.

### Rational vs. Informal Decision Making

| Mode | Rational (Ideal) | Informal (Actual) |
|---|---|---|
| **Basis** | Utility/value of outcomes + probability of occurrence | Hunches, intuition, memory, opinions of others |
| **Process** | Cool, almost mathematical | Informal, shortcut-driven |
| **Quality** | Theoretically optimal | Prone to systematic error |

---

### Heuristics: Quick but Fallible Rules of Thumb

- **Heuristics:** Mental rules of thumb that permit us to make decisions and judgments in a rapid and efficient manner (**Kahneman & Tversky**, 1982).
- Reduce effort required but may not enhance quality or accuracy.
- Extracted from past experience; serve as simple guidelines for reasonably good choices quickly and efficiently.

#### 1. Availability Heuristic

- **Definition:** A cognitive rule of thumb in which the importance or probability of various events is judged on the basis of how readily they come to mind.
- Tends to work fairly well (more frequent events are generally more easily recalled), but leads to errors.
- **Study (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974):** Lists of names with equal numbers of men and women; nearly 80% of participants said women's names appeared more frequently. Why? Because the women listed were more famous - their names were more readily recalled.
- **Real-world errors:** People overestimate chances of being a victim of violent crime, being in a plane crash, or winning the lottery - because such events receive extensive media coverage and are easy to bring to mind.

#### 2. Representativeness Heuristic

- **Definition:** A mental rule of thumb suggesting that the more closely an event or object resembles typical examples of some concept or category, the more likely it is to belong to that concept or category.
- **Example:** Describing a neat, well-read, shy, conservative-dressing neighbor - most people guess "librarian" because the traits match the prototype, ignoring **base rates**.
- **Base rates:** The relative frequency of various items or events in the external world. The representativeness heuristic causes us to ignore this potentially useful information.

#### 3. Anchoring-and-Adjustment Heuristic

- **Definition:** A cognitive rule of thumb for making decisions in which existing information is accepted as a reference point but then adjusted (usually insufficiently) in light of various factors.
- **Example:** In a market, a shopkeeper quotes an outrageous price; the buyer bargains and settles on a price that still reflects the inflated anchor. In the next shop, the settled price itself becomes the new anchor.
- **Core problem:** Adjustments are often insufficient in magnitude to offset the impact of the original reference point.

> **Exam Tip:** A table comparing all three heuristics is a likely exam question. Key: availability = ease of recall; representativeness = prototype matching; anchoring = insufficient adjustment from initial information.

---

### Escalation of Commitment: Getting Trapped in Bad Decisions

- **Escalation of Commitment:** The tendency to become increasingly committed to bad decisions even as losses associated with them increase.
- Explains why investors hold bad investments, and why people remain in troubled relationships.

#### Why It Occurs (Staw & Ross, 1989)

Three phases:

1. **Initial Phase (Rational):** Decision is based on rational considerations (expectation of gain). Temporary setbacks are common, so continuing is reasonable.
2. **Early Losses (Psychological):** As negative outcomes mount, individuals fear admitting a mistake. A growing need for **self-justification** emerges - a tendency to justify previous judgments and losses already endured (**Bobocel & Meyer**, 1994).
3. **Continuing Losses (External):** External pressures from other persons or groups affected by the decision come into play. Those who went along with the decision may block efforts to reverse it.

#### Conditions That Make Escalation Less Likely

- **Limited resources:** If losses reach preset limits, no further resources will be committed.
- **Overwhelming evidence of failure:** Clear, undeniable failure signals.
- **Diffusion of responsibility:** Less personal responsibility = less motivation to justify the mistake. Tasks of making decisions and implementing them should be assigned to different persons.
- **Accountability:** When people know they will be held accountable for consequences of their decisions.

---

### Emotions and Decision Making

- Our emotional reactions to outcomes are influenced not just by outcomes themselves but also by our **expectations** and by **counterfactual thinking**.

#### Counterfactual Thinking

- The comparison we make between the actual outcome and other outcomes that *could have* occurred.
- **Study (Mellers et al., 1997):**
  - Emotional reactions varied directly with money won or lost.
  - **Surprising wins** (low probability) were more elating than **expected wins** (high probability).
  - **Unexpected losses** produced greater disappointment than expected ones.
  - Counterfactual example: Participants who *avoided* losing were happy to receive nothing. But participants who *missed an opportunity* to win the same amount were disappointed to receive nothing - the same outcome produced different emotions depending on comparison.
- **Conclusion:** Large deviations from expected outcomes amplify joy (if positive) or disappointment (if negative). The same outcome can be elating or disappointing depending on the comparison made.

---

### Naturalistic Decision Making

- **Definition:** Decision making as it occurs in real-world settings.
- Contributions to the field:
  1. Focuses on how people bring **experience** to bear in making decisions.
  2. Broadens focus from a single decision "event" to include elements of the **decision context**.
  3. Emphasizes the **dynamic** nature of decision making and the complexity of modern environments (including costs of bad decisions in money and loss of life).
- **Environments studied:** Military settings, health care settings, the courtroom.
- **Critique of lab studies:** Laboratory tasks are often artificial and lack contextual information people have in everyday life. As people gain experience with specific judgment types, they are more likely to consider base rates. In some cases it may even be prudent to *ignore* base rates.

> **Exam Tip:** Naturalistic decision making is not just a description - it is a methodological critique of laboratory-based decision research.

## 4. Problem Solving

- **Problem Solving:** Efforts to develop or choose among various responses in order to attain desired goals.

### The 4-Stage Cycle

| Stage | Name | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Problem Identification | Recognize a problem exists; clarify issues, obstacles, and goals involved |
| **Stage 2** | Solution Generation | Formulate potential solutions; depends heavily on information in long-term memory. Wider range = better problem solving. |
| **Stage 3** | Solution Evaluation | Evaluate each alternative: Will it work? Are there obstacles? Hidden costs? |
| **Stage 4** | Implementation and Evaluation | Try potential solutions; evaluate effects. Solutions often only partially effective. |

> **Exam Tip:** Note that Stage 4 loops back - partial solutions require re-identification and re-evaluation. Problem solving is iterative, not linear.

---

### Methods of Problem Solving

#### 1. Trial and Error

- **Definition:** A method of solving problems in which possible solutions are tried until one succeeds.
- **Pros:** Simple; requires no prior information; can be the only option when insufficient information exists.
- **Cons:** Not efficient; does not guarantee a useful solution will be found.

#### 2. Algorithms

- **Definition:** A rule that guarantees a solution to a specific type of problem.
- **Pros:** Guaranteed to work if followed correctly.
- **Cons:** Time-consuming and inefficient (e.g., calling every restaurant in the phone book to find where a friend made a reservation).

#### 3. Heuristics and Analogy

- **Heuristics (in problem solving):** Strategies suggested by prior experience that may or may not work in the present case. Trade certainty for efficiency.
  - **Example:** Assuming a friend chose a restaurant close to where she lives eliminates many options and simplifies the search.
- **Analogy:** A strategy for solving problems based on applying solutions that were previously successful with other problems similar in underlying structure (**Gentner & Holyoak**, 1997).
  - **Example:** Knowing McDonald's are often near interstate highways, following signs to the interstate in an unfamiliar town.
  - People frequently use analogy without being consciously aware of it (**Burns**, 1996; **Schunn & Dunbar**, 1996).

> **Exam Tip:** Selecting an appropriate strategy is critical. Algorithms guarantee solutions but are often impractical. Heuristics are efficient but fallible. Analogy is powerful but unconscious and can misfire.

---

### Factors That Interfere with Effective Problem Solving

#### 1. Functional Fixedness

- **Definition:** The tendency to think of using objects only as they have been used in the past; the strong tendency to think of objects only in terms of their usual functions.
- **Classic demonstration (Duncker, 1945):** Candle problem - given a candle, tacks, and a box of matches, attach the candle to the wall so it burns properly. Most people think of nailing the candle or using melted wax - they fail to think of emptying the box and using it as a shelf.
- **Overcoming it:** If the matchbox is shown *empty*, people are much more likely to think of using it as a candle holder (**Weisberg & Suls**, 1973). Context cues that disrupt the usual association with an object can reduce functional fixedness.

#### 2. Mental Set

- **Definition:** The impact of past experience on present problem solving; specifically, the tendency to retain methods that were successful in the past even if better alternatives now exist.
- When mental set causes us to overlook other, more efficient approaches, it becomes costly.

> **Case Study: Luchins' Water Jar Study (1942)**
>
> Participants were given seven problems involving three jars (A, B, C) of different sizes to measure exact amounts of water. The first five problems could all be solved by the formula: Fill B, then fill A once from B and C twice from B. Participants learned this formula and applied it to all seven problems. But problem 6 had a simpler solution: Fill A, then fill C from A (done in 2 steps vs. the complex formula). Almost all participants missed it - mental set locked them into the established method. Similarly, problem 7 also has a simpler solution (A - C = goal). **Real-world parallel:** Commuters who take the same crowded route to work every day without considering faster alternatives.

> **Exam Tip:** Functional fixedness is about *objects* (seeing them only for their usual function). Mental set is about *methods* (sticking to approaches that worked in the past). Do not confuse them.
