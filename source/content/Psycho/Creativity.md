## 1. Introduction & Definitions

### Definition of Creativity

**Creativity** is formally defined by psychologists as:

> "the ability to produce work that is both **novel** (original, unexpected) and **appropriate** (it works - it is useful or meets task constraints)" *(Lubart, 1994)*

Additionally, creativity is described as the means by which people "perceive the world in unique ways and organize events in an unusual manner. It brings dignity and meaning to their lives" *(Raina, 1996)*

### Historical Context: Why Psychologists Were Initially Reluctant to Study Creativity

Two primary reasons explain this reluctance:

1. **Measurement difficulty:** Although several methods for measuring creativity existed - for instance, asking individuals to come up with as many uses for everyday objects as possible (the **Unusual Uses Test**; *Guilford, 1950*), or to formulate as many ways of improving a product as possible - these were seen as inadequate.

2. **Association with non-scientific forces:** The concept of creativity was associated, in many people's minds, with forces outside the realm of science - for instance, with vague notions of "the creative spirit." This made psychologists somewhat reluctant to study this topic.

### Definition of the Creative Process (Torrance, 1962)

**Torrance (1962)** defined creative thinking as:

> "the process of sensing gaps or disturbing, missing elements, forming ideas or hypotheses concerning them, testing the hypotheses and communicating the results, possibly modifying and retesting the hypotheses."

### Mundane Creativity vs. Exceptional Creativity

A cognitive approach to creativity calls attention to the fact that creativity is part of everyday life. A key task in the study of creativity is distinguishing between:

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mundane Creativity** | Everyday creativity that occurs with respect to problems for which past knowledge and experience give valuable clues - we know where to look for a solution | Uttering a new sentence or understanding a new concept |
| **Exceptional Creativity** | Occurs in what **Perkins (1997)** describes as **"Klondike spaces"** - areas where, like miners searching for gold in the Klondike, we do not know even where to begin looking. When a solution is found, it is dramatic and requires thinking "outside the box" - thinking that breaks out of "mental ruts" | The idea of integrated circuits, which made modern computers possible |

## 2. Stages of Creativity (Csikszentmihalyi's Model)

### Core Framework

**Csikszentmihalyi (1996)** observed that the creative process normally takes **five steps**:

### The Five Stages

| Stage | Name | Definition / Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Preparation** | Becoming immersed in problematic issues that are interesting and arouse curiosity |
| 2 | **Incubation** | Ideas churn around below the threshold of consciousness |
| 3 | **Insight** | The "Aha!" moment when the puzzle starts to fall together |
| 4 | **Evaluation** | Deciding if the insight is valuable and worth pursuing |
| 5 | **Elaboration** | Translating the insight into its final work |

### Critical Constraint: The Role of Social Valuation in the Creative Process

Csikszentmihalyi places a critical boundary condition on the outcome of this process:

> "In order to be called creative, a new meme must be socially valued. Without some form of social valuation it would be impossible to distinguish ideas that are simply bizarre from those that are genuinely creative."

Crucially, this social validation is **not** merely something that follows the individual's creative act as an afterthought. The stronger claim is:

> "there is no way, even in principle, to separate the reaction of society from the person's contribution. The two are inseparable. As long as the idea or product has not been validated, we might have **originality**, but not **creativity**."

This distinction is fundamental: **originality != creativity**. Creativity requires social validation; originality does not.

## 3. Systems Approach to Creativity

### Core Argument: Creativity as a Social and Cultural Event

The foundational challenge to purely cognitive accounts of creativity is stated directly:

> "Psychologists tend to see creativity exclusively as a mental process [but] creativity is as much a cultural and social as it is a psychological event."

The consequence of this reframing is significant:

> "Therefore, what we call creativity is **not** the product of a single individual, but of social systems making judgments about individual's products."

Furthermore:

> "Any definition of creativity that aspires to objectivity, and therefore requires an intersubjective dimension, will have to recognize the fact that the **audience** is as important to its constitution as the individual to whom it is credited."

### The Three Interacting Components

**Csikszentmihalyi's (1996) Systems Approach** proposes that creativity can be observed only at the intersection of three components:

> "Creativity is a process that can be observed only at the intersection where **individuals**, **domains**, and **fields** interact."

#### Component 1: The Person (Individual Practitioner)

- Represents the individual's **genetic makeup, talents, and experience**
- The Person **produces innovations** - novel variations in the content of the domain
- Some individuals are more likely to make such changes:
  - Either because of **personal qualities**
  - Or because they have the good fortune to be **well positioned with respect to the domain** - they have better access to it, or their social circumstances allow them free time to experiment

#### Component 2: The Domain (Cultural System)

- The Domain is the **cultural or symbolic** system
- It consists of **knowledge, tools, values, and practices**
- The Domain **transmits the existing body of knowledge** to the Person
- For creativity to occur, "a set of rules and practices must be transmitted from the domain to the individual"
- The individual must then "produce a novel variation in the content of the domain"
- **Creativity occurs when a person makes a change in a domain, a change that will be transmitted through time**

#### Component 3: The Field (Social System / Gatekeepers)

- The Field is the **social organization** of the domain
- It consists of the **teachers, critics, journal editors, museum curators, agency directors, and foundation officers** who decide what belongs to a domain and what does not
- The Field **evaluates innovations and retains selected ones**
- The Field acts as **gatekeepers**: "Changes are not adopted unless they are sanctioned by some group entitled to make decisions as to what should or should not be included in the domain"
- **Key example provided:** "In physics, the opinion of a very small number of leading university professors was enough to certify that Einstein's ideas were creative. Hundreds of millions of people accepted the judgment of this tiny field and marvelled at Einstein's creativity without understanding what it was all about."

### The Sequencing of the System

The process flows as follows:

1. The **Domain** transmits its existing body of knowledge to the **Person**
2. The **Person** produces innovations (novel variations)
3. The **Field** evaluates innovations and retains selected ones, returning them to the **Domain**
4. The validated change is then **transmitted through time** as part of the Domain

### Social Valuation: Why Originality is Not Creativity

This point deserves separate emphasis as it is the system's most philosophically significant claim:

> "most novel ideas will be quickly forgotten. Changes are not adopted unless they are sanctioned by some group entitled to make decisions as to what should or should not be included in the domain."

The inseparability claim:

> "this social validation is usually seen as something that follows the individual's creative act and can be - at least conceptually - separated from it. **The stronger claim made here is that there is no way, even in principle, to separate the reaction of society from the person's contribution. The two are inseparable.**"

### Cultural Dimensions within the Systems Approach

**Bhawuk (2003)** argued that "people strive to excel in areas which are valued in a culture." The Indian context is provided as a specific illustration: "spirituality is a cultural phenomenon valued in India even today and this continues to produce eminent spiritual gurus."

## 4. Confluence Approach

### Definition and Basic Premise

The **Confluence Approach** is defined as:

> "an approach suggesting that for creativity to occur, **multiple components must converge**." *(Amabile, 1983)*

The core claim: "only when all these conditions are present can a high level of creativity emerge." *(Sternberg & Lubart, 1996)*

### The Six Distinct Resources (Lubart, 1994)

**Lubart (1994)** specifies that creativity requires a confluence of the following six distinct resources:

| # | Resource | Detailed Description |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Intellectual Abilities** | The ability to see problems in new ways; the ability to recognize which of one's ideas are worth pursuing; and **persuasive skills** - the ability to convince others of these new ideas |
| 2 | **Knowledge** | Enough knowledge about a field to move it forward |
| 3 | **Certain Styles of Thinking** | A preference for thinking in novel ways and an ability to see the big picture - to think **globally as well as locally** |
| 4 | **Personality Attributes** | Such traits as willingness to **take risks** and to **tolerate ambiguity** |
| 5 | **Intrinsic, Task-Focused Motivation** | Creative people usually love what they are doing and find **intrinsic rewards** in their work |
| 6 | **A Supportive Environment** | An environment that is supportive of creative ideas |

### Research Evidence for the Confluence Approach

**Lubart and Sternberg (1995)** conducted a study with **48 adults** (ages 18-65) who produced creative products across **four domains**: writing, art, advertising, and science.

**Specific tasks included:**
- Art: Produce drawings showing "hope," "rage," and "the earth from an insect's point of view"
- Advertising: Design television ads for bow ties and the Internal Revenue Service

**Measures taken:** Products rated for overall creativity, novelty, and perceived effort; participants completed measures of fluid intelligence, thinking style, and two personality measures.

**Three key findings:**

1. **Inter-rater agreement:** "There was considerable agreement among raters: They agreed on what was creative and what was not creative." This is an important finding because it indicates that creativity can be studied scientifically.

2. **Domain-specificity:** "Creativity in one domain (art, writing, and so on) was only moderately related to creativity in other domains. Thus, as common sense suggests, people can be creative in one area but not in another."

3. **Convergence confirmed:** "Intellectual ability, thinking style, and personality were all significantly related to creativity."

**Conclusion:** "Creativity, it appears, requires the convergence of many factors for its emergence. None of these factors are unusual in and of themselves, but together they produce outcomes and results that are, in some cases, extraordinary."

## 5. Fostering Creativity

### The Need for Divergent Thinking

The section opens with a direct assertion:

> "To promote creativity, we need different view points. **Divergent thinking** is needed to produce as many ideas as possible."

### The 12 Techniques for Fostering Creativity (Nickerson, 1999)

**Nickerson (1999)** provides a summary of various creativity techniques developed by both academia and industry:

| # | Technique |
|---|---|
| 1 | Establishing purpose and intention |
| 2 | Building basic skills |
| 3 | Encouraging acquisitions of domain-specific knowledge |
| 4 | Stimulating and rewarding curiosity and exploration |
| 5 | Building motivation, especially **internal motivation** |
| 6 | Encouraging confidence and a willingness to take risks |
| 7 | Focusing on mastery and self-competition |
| 8 | Promoting supportable beliefs about creativity |
| 9 | Providing opportunities for choice and discovery |
| 10 | Developing self-management (**metacognitive skills**) |
| 11 | Teaching techniques and strategies for facilitating creative performance |
| 12 | Providing balance |

### Additional Techniques (Torrance, 1995)

Based on **Torrance's work (1995)**, enhancing creativity involves:

- Soft thinking
- Deconstruction / reconstruction
- Imaging
- Playing
- Exploration
- Dialectical thinking
- Learning from failure
- Collaboration / cooperation

### The Four Key Abilities in Everyday Creative Problem-Solving

Four key abilities are identified as involved in creativity, applicable to approaching everyday problems in an innovative way:

| Ability | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Fluency** | The elaboration of many ideas, alternatives, or solutions |
| **Flexibility** | The ability to process information or objects in **different ways** given the same stimulus |
| **Originality** | Involves getting away from the obvious and commonplace from habit-bound thinking |
| **Elaboration** | The ability to **embellish ideas with details** |

### Creative Thinking as a Lifestyle

The text extends creativity beyond a bounded cognitive skill:

> "Creative thinking is much more than using our fantasy to produce innovative ideas. Creative thinking can become a lifestyle, a personality trait, a way of looking at the world, a way of interacting with others, and a way of living and growing. Living creatively means developing your talents, tapping your unused potentials, and becoming what you are capable of becoming through **self-discovery and self-discipline**."

A general principle is stated: "Any time people are confronted with situations for which they have not already learned and practiced a response; some degree or kind of creativity is needed."

### Indian Context: Cultural Dimensions of Creativity

#### Integral Intelligence and Creativity

An indigenous approach to creativity in the Indian tradition is discussed under the concept of **Integral Intelligence** - a holistic framework where cognitive and non-cognitive processes are emphasized, contrasting with Western technological intelligence.

**Misra, Srivastava, and Misra (2006)** speak of "Indian thought systems and Indian intuitive creativity with a special emphasis on the cultural variation based on conceptualization, manifestation, and societal organization."

#### Gender, Sensitivity, and Independence

The text identifies a structural cultural tension at the heart of Indian creative expression:

> "Creativity by its very nature requires **sensitivity** and **independence** and in the Indian tradition, sensitivity is a **feminine virtue** while independence is a **masculine one**. Cultural emphasis on sex is an important source of conflict for developing creativity."

**Empirical evidence on gender differences in creativity is mixed:**

| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| No gender difference in creativity | Das (1996); Kapoor (1996); Verma (1993) |
| Boys are more creative than girls | Agarwal & Agarwal (1999); Hussain & Sinha (1995) |

#### Cultural Values and Creative Domain Selection

**Bhawuk (2003)** argued that "people strive to excel in areas which are valued in a culture." The example given is spirituality:

> "spirituality is a cultural phenomenon valued in India even today and this continues to produce eminent spiritual gurus."

This illustrates that the **domain** in which creativity is expressed is not culturally neutral - the social valuation of certain domains over others directly shapes where creative energy is invested and recognized.
