## 1. What is Intelligence?

**Intelligence** (Neisser et al., 1996): Individuals' abilities to:
- Understand complex ideas
- Adapt effectively to the environment
- Learn from experience
- Engage in various forms of reasoning
- Overcome obstacles by careful thought

Intelligence is related to important life outcomes (school success, job performance, social functioning), but the relationship is imperfect - many other factors play a role.

---

# 2. Theories of Intelligence: Unitary vs. Multifaceted

## 2.1 The Core Debate

| Dimension | Unitary View | Multifaceted View |
|---|---|---|
| Core Claim | One general factor (g) drives all cognitive performance | Multiple independent abilities; a person can be high on some, low on others |
| Key Supporter | Spearman (1927) | Thurstone (1938), Gardner (1983), Sternberg (1985) |
| Evidence | Test scores across domains correlate highly with each other | Clinical cases (brain damage spares specific functions while impairing others) |
| Criticism | Cannot explain why frontal lobe damage leaves IQ intact; cannot explain savant-like profiles | Complex; harder to measure with a single score |

## 2.2 Spearman's g Factor (1927)

- Performance on any cognitive task depends on a **primary general factor (g)** plus task-specific factors.
- Evidence: scores across different intelligence subtests correlate highly, suggesting a common underlying factor.
- Method: **factor analysis** - identifies clusters of related items on a test.

## 2.3 Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities (1938)

Seven distinct abilities:
1. **Verbal meaning** - understanding ideas and word meanings
2. **Number** - speed and accuracy with numbers
3. **Space** - ability to visualize objects in 3D
4. (Plus four additional abilities)

# 3. Major Modern Theories of Intelligence

## 3.1 Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences (1983)

### Background
Gardner argued that studying only "average" individuals was too limiting. He proposed studying:
- Acclaimed geniuses
- Individuals with cognitive impairments
- Domain experts and those with special mental "gifts"

### The Intelligences

| Intelligence Type | Description / Example |
|---|---|
| Verbal / Linguistic | Understanding of word meanings and ideas |
| Mathematical / Logical | Speed and accuracy with numbers and logic |
| Spatial | Ability to visualize objects in three dimensions |
| **Musical** | Playing any tune on the piano without formal training |
| **Bodily-Kinesthetic** | Complex physical feats - e.g., Olympic athletes |
| **Personal / Interpersonal** | Ability to get along well with others (linked to EQ) |

> Key Point: Gardner added musical, bodily-kinesthetic, and personal intelligences to the traditional verbal/mathematical/spatial set.

### Key Takeaways - Gardner
- Intelligence is not one thing; there are several important types.
- Athletic excellence IS a form of intelligence (bodily-kinesthetic).
- You must understand each type to get the full picture of human intelligence.
- Method: studying extremes (genius AND impaired) revealed forms of intelligence traditional tests missed.

---

## 3.2 Sternberg's Triarchic Theory (1985, 1995)

**Triarchic Theory**: A theory suggesting there are **three basic forms of intelligence**: componential, experiential, and contextual.

| Type | Also Called | Core Ability | Who Excels |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Componential** | Analytic | Think critically and analytically | Students; high scorers on standard academic tests |
| **Experiential** | Creative | Insight; formulating new ideas; combining unrelated facts | Scientific geniuses, inventors (Einstein, Newton, Freud) |
| **Contextual** | Practical | Solving everyday problems; "street smarts"; adaptive | People who are practically effective in real-world settings |

**Practical Intelligence**: Intelligence useful in solving everyday problems.

> Key Point: Practical intelligence requires different skills from academic intelligence. High practical intelligence does NOT guarantee high scores on standard IQ tests.

### Key Takeaways - Sternberg
- IQ tests primarily measure componential (analytic) intelligence only.
- Success in life - especially business - requires practical (contextual) intelligence that standard tests do not capture.
- The three types are relatively independent.

---

## 3.3 Cattell's Theory of Fluid and Crystallized Intelligence (1963)

Both types identified via **factor analysis**.

| Type | Definition | Example | Age Trajectory |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fluid Intelligence** | Largely inherited ability to think and reason; hardware of the brain; limits information-processing capacity | Speed of information analysis | **Decreases slowly with age** |
| **Crystallized Intelligence** | Accumulated knowledge; information stored over a lifetime of experience; application of skills to specific problems | Breadth of vocabulary; how many words one can use | **Stays level or increases with age** |

Relationship: Crystallized intelligence is the **outcome of experience acting on fluid intelligence**.

> Critical Implication: Older individuals can sometimes outperform younger ones because declines in fluid intelligence are offset by the vast store of crystallized knowledge. Explains why experienced scientists and chess players remain competitive.

### Key Takeaways - Cattell
- Fluid = nature/hardware; Crystallized = nurture/software.
- Aging does not uniformly reduce intelligence - it shifts the balance between the two types.

---

## 3.4 PASS Theory of Intelligence (Das, Naglieri, and Kirby, 1994)

**P.A.S.S.** = Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, Successive

Motivation: Clinical cases show brain damage can spare IQ while impairing higher cognition (frontal lobe damage). Dyslexic children can have high IQs. This challenges a single-g view.

### The Four Processes

| Process | Brain Region | Core Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | Frontal lobe (prefrontal dorsolateral cortex for reasoning; fronto-orbital for social decisions) | Goal setting, decision-making, monitoring feedback, guiding attention |
| **Attention / Arousal** | Brain stem (arousal); frontal lobes (attentional mobilization); thalamus (inhibitory) | Selective attention; resisting distractions; maintaining vigilance |
| **Simultaneous Processing** | Occipital and parietal lobes | Integrating stimuli into whole groups; each piece related to others |
| **Successive Processing** | Frontal temporal lobe | Integrating stimuli in a specific serial order (e.g., understanding grammar) |

**Knowledge Base**: All four processes float on a "sea of knowledge." Two types:
- **Tacit**: Spontaneous, experiential
- **Explicit**: Formal, instructed

**Output Modes** (Donald, 1991): Movements, mimetics (dance, music, body language), language.

**Practical Applications**:
- Cognitive Assessment System (CAS; Naglieri & Das, 1997)
- PASS Reading Enhancement Program (PREP; Das, 1999)
- Program for remediating arithmetic difficulties (linked to planning deficits)

---

## 3.5 Culture and Intelligence

**Vygotsky's Position**: Culture provides the social context in which intelligence develops. Higher mental functions (problem solving, thinking) are largely culture-produced.

| Culture Type | Valued Intelligence | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Technologically advanced (Western) | Generalization, abstraction, speed, analysis, individual achievement | **Technological Intelligence** |
| Non-Western (Asian, African) | Social/emotional skills, relating to others, self-reflection, collectivist orientation | (No single label; contrasts with technological) |

**Technological Intelligence**: Advanced societies adopt child-rearing practices that enhance skills of attention, observation, analysis, performance, speed, and achievement orientation. Western IQ tests measure this type.

---

## 3.6 Intelligence in the Indian Tradition

**Integral Intelligence**: A holistic approach where equal attention is paid to cognitive AND non-cognitive processes and their integration.

- Sanskrit term **Buddhi** (J.P. Das, 1994): Far broader than Western intelligence. Includes mental effort, determined action, feelings, opinions, knowledge, discrimination, understanding, conscience, will, desire.
- Intelligence is treated as a **state, a process, and an entity** (Srivastava & Misra, 2001, 2007).
- Expression depends on one's own **effort, persistence, and motivation**.

### Four Competencies (Srivastava & Misra, 2007)

1. **Cognitive competence**: Context sensitivity, understanding, discrimination, problem solving, communication.
2. **Social competence**: Respect for social order, commitment to elders/needy, concern for others, recognizing others' perspectives.
3. **Emotional competence**: Self-regulation, honesty, politeness, good conduct, self-evaluation.
4. **Entrepreneurial competence**: Commitment, persistence, patience, hard work, vigilance, goal-directed behavior.

**Sri Aurobindo**: Ultimate aim of intelligence = direct cognizance without sensory mediation or ego distortion. Higher function = self-awareness.

**Krishnamurti**: Intelligence is truth, beauty, completeness, and love itself.

# 4. Measuring Intelligence

## 4.1 The Stanford-Binet Intelligence Test

**Origin**: Alfred Binet and Theodore Simon, commissioned by Paris school board in **1904** to identify children needing special education.

**Design Principles**:
- Items answerable without special training or study.
- Two types of items: (a) so new/unusual no child had prior exposure; (b) so familiar nearly all would have encountered them.
- Sample tasks: Follow simple commands; name objects in pictures; repeat 15-word sentences; explain how two objects differ; complete sentences.

**First version**: Published **1905**, 30 items.
**Revised version**: Published **1908**, items grouped by age (age 3-13), 6 items per level. Items placed at an age level if ~75% of children that age passed correctly.

**Stanford-Binet Test** (Lewis Terman, Stanford University): American adaptation. **Stanford-Binet Test**: A widely used individual test of intelligence.

### IQ: Original Definition
**IQ (Intelligence Quotient)** = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) x 100

- Equal mental and chronological age = IQ of **100** (average).
- IQ > 100: more intelligent than typical peers.
- IQ < 100: less intelligent than peers.

**Problem with original formula**: Mental growth levels off in the early teens, but chronological age keeps increasing, causing IQ scores to decline artificially after adolescence.

### IQ: Modern Definition
IQ now simply reflects **an individual's performance relative to persons of the same age** who have taken the same test.

---

## 4.2 The Wechsler Scales (David Wechsler)

Developed to overcome Stanford-Binet limitations. Includes both **verbal** and **non-verbal (performance)** subtests, yielding separate scores.

### WAIS-3 (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale - Revised) Subtests

**Verbal Tests**:

| Subtest | Description |
|---|---|
| Information | General knowledge questions, increasing difficulty |
| Digit Span | Repeat series of digits read aloud by examiner |
| Vocabulary | Define 35 words |
| Arithmetic | Solve arithmetic problems |
| Comprehension | Answer questions requiring detailed answers |
| Similarities | Indicate in what way two items are alike |

**Performance Tests**:

| Subtest | Description |
|---|---|
| Picture Completion | Identify missing part of a picture |
| Picture Arrangement | Arrange pictures to make a sensible story |
| Block Design | Duplicate designs made with red and white blocks |
| Object Assembly | Solve picture puzzles |
| Digit Symbol | Fill boxes with coded symbols matching a number above |

**WISC** (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children): Used to identify learning disabilities. Children scoring high on Picture Completion and Object Assembly but low on Arithmetic, Information, and Vocabulary are more likely to have learning disabilities (Aiken, 1991).

**Caveat**: Wechsler developed these tests before the multifaceted nature of intelligence was well understood; it is not fully clear his subtests measure distinct aspects of intelligence.

---

## 4.3 Raven Progressive Matrices (Raven, 1977)

**Purpose**: A culture-fair intelligence test.

**Structure**: 60 matrices of varying difficulty; each contains a logical pattern or design with a missing part. Individuals select the item that completes the pattern from several choices.

**Focus**: Primarily on **fluid intelligence** - forming concepts, reasoning, identifying similarities.

**Why culture-fair**: Nonverbal in nature; designed for those exposed to languages other than standard English; avoids culturally specific knowledge.

**Limitation**: Does not completely eliminate subtle built-in bias.

---

## 4.4 Aptitude vs. Achievement Tests

| Type | Definition | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aptitude Tests** | Measure ability to acquire new information | Predict future performance | WAIS, Stanford-Binet, college entrance exams |
| **Achievement Tests** | Measure what has already been learned | Reflect current performance and past learning | Classroom exams |

Note: The distinction is not perfectly precise - aptitude tests (e.g., SAT) often include vocabulary, which reflects past learning.

---

## 4.5 Group Tests of Intelligence

Motivated by WWI: U.S. armed forces needed to screen millions of recruits rapidly.

Arthur Otis developed:
- **Army Alpha**: For persons who could read (1917).
- **Army Beta**: For persons who could not read or did not speak English.

Used to select officer training candidates; accurately predicted success in training.

---

## 4.6 The Cognitive Basis of Intelligence: Processing Speed

**Processing Speed**: The speed at which individuals perform simple perceptual and cognitive tasks.

- Correlates significantly with IQ test scores (Neisser et al., 1996; Vernon, 1987).
- Reaction time measures correlate **-0.30 to -0.40** with intelligence scores.

**Inspection Time**: The **minimum amount of time** a particular stimulus must be exposed for individuals to make a judgment about it that meets some preestablished criterion of accuracy (e.g., 85%).

- Procedure: Show simple drawings; ask if the longer side is left or right; participants instructed to be accurate, not fast.
- Correlates **-0.50 or more** with standard intelligence test scores (Kranzler & Jensen, 1989).
- Measures: The amount of time required for intake of new visual information.

---

## 4.7 Neural Basis of Intelligence

**Nerve Conduction Velocity**: Speed of nerve impulses in the visual system correlates significantly with intelligence measures (Reed & Jensen, 1993).

**Metabolic Efficiency**: More intelligent people's brains expend **less energy** during complex cognitive tasks (Haier et al., 1993). Measured by PET brain imaging.

**Brain Structure**: Scores on WAIS correlate with size of left and right **temporal lobes** and left and right **hippocampus**, even after correcting for overall body size (Andreason et al., 1993).

# 5. Psychometric Requirements: Standardization, Reliability, and Validity

## 5.1 Standardization

A test must be administered to a **large representative sample** of the population it is designed for. The mean and distribution of scores are then examined.

A well-constructed test produces a **normal (bell-shaped) curve**:
- Most scores cluster in the middle.
- Fewer scores occur at the extremes.
- Average IQ is set at **100**.

The normal curve allows determination of where any individual stands relative to all others who took the test (percentile ranking).

---

## 5.2 Reliability

**Reliability**: The extent to which any measuring device (including psychological tests) yields the **same result each time** it is applied to the same quantity.

Two forms:

### Internal Consistency (Split-Half Reliability)
**Split-Half Reliability**: The **correlation between scores on two parts of a test** (e.g., odd vs. even items, or first vs. second half).

- If the test reliably measures intelligence, the correlation between halves should be high and positive.
- Low correlation = some items may measure different things = unreliable.

### Test-Retest Reliability
**Test-Retest Reliability**: A measure of the extent to which scores on a test **remain stable over time**.

- Same individuals take the test at different times.
- Higher similarity of scores across occasions = higher test-retest reliability.
- Intelligence is not expected to change dramatically over time, so high test-retest reliability is essential.

---

## 5.3 Validity

**Validity**: The extent to which a test **actually measures what it claims to measure**.

### Content Validity
**Content Validity**: The extent to which items on a test are **related in a straightforward way** to the characteristic the test aims to measure.

Example of low content validity: An "intelligence test" that measures ear length or tooth sharpness - clearly unrelated to intelligence.

### Criterion-Related Validity
**Criterion-Related Validity**: The extent to which scores on a test are **related to behaviors (criteria) that are relevant** to the characteristics the test purports to measure.

Two subtypes:

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Concurrent Validity** | Test scores correlate with relevant behaviors **right now** |
| **Predictive Validity** | Test scores predict relevant behaviors **in the future** |

Example: IQ scores should predict school grades (concurrent) and career success (predictive).

### How Well Do Intelligence Tests Hold Up?

| Criterion | Rating |
|---|---|
| Reliability | High - consistent scores, good internal consistency |
| Validity for school performance | High |
| Validity for fields requiring academic skill (medicine, law, science, engineering) | Moderate |
| Validity for business success | Low |
| Validity for success as musician, executive, politician | Very low |

**Key limitation**: Standard tests were designed to measure componential/analytic intelligence - NOT practical intelligence. Persons high in practical intelligence do not necessarily score high on standard IQ tests.

---

## 5.4 Competency Assessment

An alternative to standard IQ testing for predicting career success.

**Method**: Behavioral-event interviews comparing known high performers vs. average performers. Differences found = **competencies**.

**Key competencies for executive success**: Inductive reasoning; analytical thinking; strong desire for success; flexibility; wanting impact on others; willingness to take initiative; self-confidence; good understanding of others (social skills).

**Validation** (McClelland, 1998): Executives classified as highly qualified via competency assessment received higher bonuses and had 0% turnover (vs. ~50% turnover for traditionally hired executives). Saved the company >$4 million in one year.

Not ALL competencies are required; roughly **five** are sufficient for high success.

# 6. Human Intelligence: Heredity vs. Environment

## 6.1 Evidence for Heredity

**IQ Correlations by Relationship**:

| Relationship | IQ Correlation |
|---|---|
| Identical twins raised together | ~+0.90 |
| Brothers and sisters | ~+0.50 |
| Cousins | ~+0.15 |

**Colorado Adoption Project** (Plomin et al., 1997): 245 adopted children studied from 29 days old to adolescence.
- Correlation between adopted child's IQ and **biological parents' IQ increased over time**.
- Correlation between adopted child's IQ and **adoptive parents' IQ decreased over time**.
- Same pattern for specific components of intelligence.
- Caveat: adoptive homes were above average SES, so environmental deprivation effects were not tested.

**Quantitative Trait Loci (QTLs)**: Genes with small individual effects that influence likelihood of a characteristic. Many genes each contributing slightly to general intelligence (Rutter & Plomin, 1997; Chorney et al., 1998 - one gene accounted for only ~2% of variance in general intelligence).

**Identical Twins Reared Apart** (Bouchard et al., 1990): IQs of twins separated at birth (often within days) correlate almost as highly as twins raised together. Also similar in physical appearance, preferences, mannerisms, and personality.

**Heritability**: The proportion of variance in a trait within a given population **attributable to genetic factors**.
- Ranges from ~35% in childhood to ~75% in adulthood (McGue et al., 1993).
- Overall estimate: ~50% (Plomin et al., 1997).
- Increases with age because older individuals increasingly choose/shape their own environments to match genetic tendencies.

## 6.2 Evidence for Environment

**Flynn Effect** (Flynn, 1987): IQ test performance has risen **~3 points per decade worldwide** at all age levels. Requires periodic restandardization of tests. Cannot be explained by genetic shifts between generations; must be environmental.

Suggested environmental contributors: better nutrition, urbanization, television, more/better education, more cognitively demanding jobs, computer games.

**Environmental Deprivation Studies**: Absence of key environmental stimulation early in life reduces intelligence (Gottfried, 1984).

**Skeels (1938, 1966) Enrichment Study**:
- 13 children (~2 years old) removed from stimulus-deprived orphanage and placed with women in an institution.
- After a few years: IQs rose ~29 points (corrected to ~10-13 points; Flynn, 1993).
- Children left in orphanage: IQs dropped ~26 points.
- 25 years later: enriched-environment group doing well (graduated, employed, married). Control group institutionalized or functioning poorly.

**Indian context** (Misra & Tripathi, 2004): Negative effects of poverty and deprivation on intelligence become more pronounced with age - cumulative deficit.

**Conditions for Effective Intervention** (Ramey & Ramey, 1998):
1. Begin early, continue long-term.
2. Intense - home visits several times per week.
3. Direct learning experiences from experts (not just through parents).
4. Broad scope; multiple procedures.
5. Matched to individual child's needs.
6. Environmental supports in place (e.g., quality schools).

**Biological/environmental hazards that reduce IQ**: Prolonged malnutrition; lead exposure (air or paint); prenatal alcohol and drug exposure.

# 7. Group Differences in Intelligence Test Scores

## 7.1 Environmental Explanation (Mainstream View)

**Cultural Bias**: The tendency of items on a test of intelligence to **require specific cultural experience or knowledge**.

- Tests standardized largely on middle-class white populations.
- Some items more familiar to majority group children, giving them an advantage.
- Minority children may feel threatened by unfamiliar items, further reducing performance (Steele & Aronson, 1996).

**Culture-Fair Tests**: Attempt to include items to which all groups have been equally exposed; tend to be nonverbal. Example: **Raven Progressive Matrices**.

**Flynn's Direct Evidence**: African American soldiers' children raised by white mothers in Germany had IQs virtually identical to white children matched on SES (Flynn, 1980). When environmental differences are removed, group differences disappear.

**Analogy** (Figure 11.9): Genetically identical seeds planted in poor vs. fertile soil grow differently - differences between fields = environmental; differences within each field = genetic. Same logic applies to group IQ differences.

## 7.2 Genetic Hypothesis (Minority View)

**The Bell Curve** (Herrnstein & Murray, 1994): Argued for genetic factors in group IQ differences; suggested IQ may not be modifiable through environment; opposed special programs for disadvantaged minorities.

**Criticism of Bell Curve** (Sternberg, 1995; Schultze, Kane, & Dickens, 1996): High heritability within a group does NOT imply genetic explanation for differences between groups - only valid if group environments are identical (they are not). Most psychologists reject the genetic hypothesis for group differences.

## 7.3 Gender Differences in Intelligence

Overall: Males and females score virtually identically on standard intelligence tests.

**Subtle differences**:

| Domain | Advantage | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal abilities (synonyms, fluency), literature, spelling, writing | Females | Small differences; decreasing over time (Feingold, 1992) |
| Visual-spatial tasks (mental rotation, tracking moving objects) | Males | Small differences; far smaller than stereotypes suggest |
| Object location memory (noticing/remembering objects in a room) | Females | Larger difference when NOT told to memorize |
| Navigation / route-finding over large distances | Males | |

**Evolutionary explanation** (Silverman & Eals, 1992): Reflects sex-differentiated hunter-gatherer tasks. Women (gatherers) needed to notice and relocate plants. Men (hunters) needed to navigate back home over large distances.

Note: Cannot be tested experimentally; remains speculative.

# 8. Emotional Intelligence: The Feeling Side of Intelligence

## 8.1 Definition

**Emotional Intelligence (EQ)** (Goleman, 1995, 1998): A cluster of traits or abilities relating to the **emotional side of life** - recognizing and managing one's own emotions, motivating oneself, restraining impulses, recognizing and managing others' emotions, and handling interpersonal relationships effectively.

Goleman argues EQ is **more important for a happy, productive life** than IQ.

## 8.2 Two Research Models

### Ability Model (Mayer, Caruso, and Salovey, 2000)
EQ as ability to **process information** about emotions.

Four components:
1. Emotional perception
2. Emotional integration
3. Emotional understanding
4. Emotional management

Measurement: **Multifactor Emotional Intelligence Scale**.

### Personality Model (Baron, 1997)
EQ as personality trait.

Five components:
1. Intrapersonal
2. Interpersonal
3. Adaptability
4. Stress management
5. Mood

## 8.3 Goleman's Five Major Components (1995)

### 1. Knowing Our Own Emotions
- Awareness of one's own feelings and thoughts about them.
- Without self-awareness: cannot make intelligent life choices; low expressiveness; others cannot read emotional state.

### 2. Managing Our Own Emotions
- Regulating the nature, intensity, and expression of emotions.
- Critical for mental health and interpersonal effectiveness.
- Inability to control temper = avoidance by others; poor career and relationship outcomes.

### 3. Motivating Ourselves
- Ability to motivate oneself to work long and hard.
- Remaining enthusiastic and optimistic.
- **Delay of gratification**: putting off small rewards now for larger rewards later (Shoda, Mischel, & Peake, 1990).

### 4. Recognizing and Influencing Others' Emotions
- Accurately reading others' moods.
- Practical use: knowing the right time to ask for a favor.
- Generating desired emotions in others = highly effective in sales, politics.

### 5. Handling Relationships (Interpersonal Intelligence; Hatch, 1990)
- Coordinating group efforts; negotiating interpersonal solutions.
- Giving feedback without provoking anger or resentment.
- Being a team player.

EQ can be enhanced through training in: self-monitoring, self-regulation, communication, and problem solving.

## 8.4 Indian Context of EQ (Sibia, Srivastava, and Misra, 2003)

- Embedded in social concerns, virtues, religious traditions, and cultural practices.
- The emotionally regulated person = **Jitendriya**.
- Context-sensitive; emphasizes role of significant others (guru, family, society).
- **Bhagavad Gita**: Addresses emotional crisis and managing emotions to fulfill one's duty.
- Indian EQ = using emotions to do what is right and fulfill one's duties.

# 9. Creativity: Generating the Extraordinary

## 9.1 Definition

**Creativity**: The ability to produce work that is both **novel** (original, unexpected) and **appropriate** (it works - it is useful or meets task constraints) (Lubart, 1994).

**Torrance (1962)**: Creative thinking is the process of sensing gaps or disturbing/missing elements, forming and testing hypotheses concerning them, communicating the results, possibly modifying and retesting hypotheses.

Key abilities in creativity:
- **Fluency**: Generating many ideas/alternatives/solutions.
- **Flexibility**: Processing information in different ways given the same stimulus.
- **Originality**: Getting away from the obvious; breaking habit-bound thinking.
- **Elaboration**: Embellishing ideas with details.

## 9.2 Mundane vs. Exceptional Creativity (Perkins, 1997)

| Type | When It Occurs | Characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| **Mundane creativity** | Everyday - uttering new sentences, understanding new concepts | Past knowledge provides clues; we know where to look |
| **Exceptional creativity** | Rare breakthroughs | "Klondike spaces" - we don't know where to look; "outside the box" thinking |

## 9.3 Contrasting Views of Creativity

**Cognitive approach**: Retrieval from memory, association, synthesis, transformation, and categorical reduction underlie creative thought.

**Social psychology approach**: Focuses on personality traits that make people creative and environmental conditions that encourage or discourage creativity (Simonton, 1994).

**Motivational approach**: Intrinsic, task-focused motivation sustained without external rewards.

## 9.4 Systems Approach (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996)

Creativity is not purely individual; it is **social and cultural** as well as psychological.

Three components:
- **Person**: Individual with genetic makeup, talents, experience.
- **Domain**: Cultural system - knowledge, tools, values, practices transmitted to the individual.
- **Field**: Social system - teachers, critics, editors, curators, officers who **evaluate and select** innovations. "Gatekeepers."

> "Creativity is a process that can be observed only at the intersection where individuals, domains, and fields interact."

**Without social validation**: You may have originality, but NOT creativity.

**Five Steps of the Creative Process** (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996):
1. **Preparation**: Immersion in interesting problems that arouse curiosity.
2. **Incubation**: Ideas churn below the threshold of consciousness.
3. **Insight**: The "Aha!" moment when the puzzle falls together.
4. **Evaluation**: Deciding if the insight is valuable and worth pursuing.
5. **Elaboration**: Translating the insight into its final work.

## 9.5 Confluence Approach (Lubart, 1994; Amabile, 1983)

**Confluence Approach**: For creativity to occur, **multiple components must converge**.

Six required resources (Lubart, 1994):
1. **Intellectual abilities**: Seeing problems in new ways; recognizing which ideas are worth pursuing; persuasion.
2. **Knowledge**: Enough domain knowledge to move it forward.
3. **Thinking styles**: Preference for novel thinking; ability to see the big picture (global AND local).
4. **Personality attributes**: Willingness to take risks; tolerance for ambiguity.
5. **Intrinsic, task-focused motivation**: Love of the work; intrinsic rewards.
6. **Supportive environment**: Context that supports creative ideas.

**Evidence** (Lubart & Sternberg, 1995): 48 adults produced creative products in writing, art, advertising, and science.
- Raters showed considerable agreement on what was creative.
- Creativity in one domain only moderately correlated with creativity in another (domain-specific).
- Intellectual ability, thinking style, and personality all significantly correlated with creativity.

**Indian Creativity**: Spirituality is culturally valued in India and produces eminent spiritual gurus. Sensitivity (feminine virtue) and independence (masculine virtue) are sources of creative tension (Misra, Srivastava, & Misra, 2006).

## 9.6 Fostering Creativity (Nickerson, 1999)

1. Establishing purpose and intention
2. Building basic skills
3. Encouraging domain-specific knowledge
4. Stimulating and rewarding curiosity and exploration
5. Building motivation (especially internal)
6. Encouraging confidence and risk-taking
7. Focusing on mastery and self-competition
8. Promoting supportable beliefs about creativity
9. Providing choice and discovery opportunities
10. Developing self-management (metacognitive skills)
11. Teaching creativity strategies
12. Providing balance

**Torrance (1995)**: Soft thinking, deconstruction/reconstruction, imaging, playing, exploration, dialectical thinking, learning from failure, collaboration/cooperation.

| **Practical Intelligence** | Intelligence useful in solving everyday problems ("street smarts") |
| **Fluid Intelligence** | Inherited abilities to think and reason; declines with age |
| **Crystallized Intelligence** | Accumulated knowledge and skills; stays level or increases with age |
| **Technological Intelligence** | Type of intelligence valued in Western societies; emphasizes analysis, speed, achievement |
| **Integral Intelligence** | Indian holistic concept balancing cognitive and non-cognitive processes |
| **Buddhi** | Sanskrit term; broader than Western intelligence; includes cognitive, affective, and motivational components |
| **Stanford-Binet Test** | A widely used individual test of intelligence |
| **IQ** | Originally mental age/chronological age x 100; now performance relative to age peers |
| **Mental Retardation** | Considerably below-average intellectual functioning combined with difficulty meeting daily life demands |
| **Down Syndrome** | Genetically caused condition (extra chromosome) resulting in mental retardation |
| **Raven Progressive Matrices** | Culture-fair test; focuses on fluid intelligence; 60 matrix items with missing parts |
| **Heritability** | Proportion of variance in a trait within a population attributable to genetic factors |
| **Flynn Effect** | Worldwide rise in IQ test scores (~3 points/decade); attributed to environmental factors |
| **Aptitude Tests** | Measure ability to acquire new information; predict future performance |
| **Achievement Tests** | Measure current knowledge from prior learning |
| **Reliability** | Extent to which a measuring device yields the same result each time |
| **Split-Half Reliability** | Correlation between scores on two halves of a test |
| **Test-Retest Reliability** | Stability of test scores over time |
| **Validity** | Extent to which a test measures what it claims to measure |
| **Content Validity** | Items directly and obviously related to the characteristic being measured |
| **Criterion-Related Validity** | Scores correlate with relevant behavioral criteria |
| **Concurrent Validity** | Test scores match current behavioral criteria |
| **Predictive Validity** | Test scores predict future behavioral criteria |
| **Inspection Time** | Minimum stimulus exposure needed for accurate judgment; correlates -.50+ with IQ |
| **Emotional Intelligence (EQ)** | Cluster of abilities relating to emotional side of life; recognizing, managing, using emotions |
| **Creativity** | Production of work that is both novel and appropriate |
| **Confluence Approach** | Multiple components must converge for creativity to emerge |
| **Cultural Bias** | Test items requiring cultural experience that advantages certain groups over others |
| **Genetic Hypothesis** | View that group IQ differences are due at least partly to genetic factors (minority view) |
