# PART 1: INTELLIGENCE - CONTRASTING VIEWS OF ITS NATURE

## 1.1 Core Definition

**Intelligence** (Neisser et al., 1996): Individuals' abilities to:
- Understand complex ideas
- Adapt effectively to the environment
- Learn from experience
- Engage in various forms of reasoning
- Overcome obstacles by careful thought

> NOTE: Intelligence is RELATED to important life outcomes (school success, job success, social functioning), but the relationship is far from perfect. Many other factors play a role. Predictions based on intelligence alone can be wrong.

---

## 1.2 Unitary vs. Multifaceted: The Core Debate

| Dimension | Unitary View | Multifaceted View |
|---|---|---|
| Core claim | One general factor underlies all cognitive ability | Multiple independent abilities that can vary within a person |
| Key supporter | Spearman (1927) | Thurstone (1938); Gardner (1983); Sternberg (1985) |
| Evidence | High inter-item correlations on IQ tests | Brain damage can impair specific functions while sparing others; savant syndromes |
| Central construct | **g** (general factor) | Multiple distinct intelligences or components |
| Weakness | Ignores domain-specific talents; doesn't explain savants | Risk of inflating the concept of "intelligence" to mean almost anything |

---

## 1.3 Spearman's g Factor (1927)

- Performance on ANY cognitive task depends on:
  1. A primary **general factor (g)**
  2. One or more **specific factors** tied to particular tasks
- Evidence: Items on intelligence tests, despite measuring different things, **correlate highly with each other** -- suggesting a single underlying dimension.
- Method: **Factor analysis** (identifies clusters of correlated items)

**Key Takeaway:** Spearman believed inter-task correlation proved a unitary g. He is the founding father of the unitary view.

---

## 1.4 Thurstone's Primary Mental Abilities (1938)

Proposed 7 distinct primary mental abilities:
1. **Verbal meaning** - understanding ideas and word meanings
2. **Number** - speed and accuracy with numbers
3. **Space** - visualizing objects in 3D
4. (+ 4 additional abilities not individually named in this text)

**Key Takeaway:** Thurstone is the early champion of the multifaceted view. A given person can be high on some abilities and low on others.

# PART 2: THREE MAJOR MODERN THEORIES

## 2.1 Gardner's Theory of Multiple Intelligences (1983)

### Philosophy Behind the Theory
Gardner argued that the traditional focus on "normal" populations **limited** psychology's view of intelligence. He proposed studying:
- Acclaimed geniuses (at the high extreme)
- Cognitively impaired individuals (at the low extreme)
- Experts in various domains
- Persons with special mental "gifts"

This broader lens revealed forms of intelligence the standard tests completely missed.

### The Types of Intelligence (Gardner's List)

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Verbal/Linguistic** | Understanding ideas, word meanings | Strong writers, orators |
| **Mathematical/Logical** | Numerical and logical reasoning | Mathematicians, scientists |
| **Spatial** | Visualizing objects in 3D | Architects, sculptors |
| **Musical** | Perceiving, producing, transforming music | Musicians; playing any tune by ear without training |
| **Bodily-Kinesthetic** | Using the body with skill and precision | Olympic athletes, dancers |
| **Personal/Interpersonal** | Getting along well with others; understanding others' emotions | Leaders, therapists |
| **Intrapersonal** | Understanding one's own emotions and motivations | (linked to EQ, discussed later) |
| **Naturalist** | Recognizing and classifying natural objects | Biologists, naturalists |

> EXAM NOTE: The text specifically highlights **Musical** and **Bodily-Kinesthetic** as examples Gardner added to the traditional verbal/math/spatial triumvirate. Personal intelligence is highlighted as overlapping with emotional intelligence.

### Key Takeaway - Gardner
> Several important types of intelligence exist. Understanding ALL of them is required to understand the full picture of human intelligence. Athletic brilliance is as legitimate a form of intelligence as verbal fluency.

---

## 2.2 Sternberg's Triarchic Theory (1985; Sternberg et al., 1995)

**Triarchic Theory:** A theory suggesting there are **three** basic forms of intelligence: componential, experiential, and contextual.

### The Three Components

#### Component 1: Componential (Analytic) Intelligence
- Involves: Ability to **think critically and analytically**
- Who excels: Persons who score high on **standard tests of academic potential**; excellent students
- Context: Succeeds in school and academic environments

#### Component 2: Experiential (Creative) Intelligence
- Involves: **Insight** and the ability to **formulate new ideas**
- Who excels: Persons who can zero in on **crucial information** in a situation; who can **combine seemingly unrelated facts**
- Examples: Einstein, Newton, Freud
- Context: Scientific discovery, invention

#### Component 3: Contextual (Practical) Intelligence
- **Definition (Practical Intelligence):** Intelligence useful in solving everyday problems.
- Involves: Being intelligent in a **practical, adaptive sense**
- Colloquial term: **"Street smarts"**
- Who excels: Persons adept at solving the **problems of everyday life**
- Key insight (Sternberg): Solving practical problems requires a **DIFFERENT** kind of intelligence from that required for school success. Practical intelligence and academic intelligence are NOT the same thing.

### Key Takeaway - Sternberg
> High IQ does not guarantee street smarts. A person can be analytically brilliant but contextually/practically inept, and vice versa. Three distinct intelligence types operate somewhat independently.

---

## 2.3 Cattell's Theory of Fluid and Crystallized Intelligence (1963)

Method: Cattell used **factor analysis** to identify two major clusters of mental ability.

### The Two Types

| Dimension | Fluid Intelligence | Crystallized Intelligence |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Largely **inherited** abilities to think and reason | **Accumulated knowledge** -- information stored over a lifetime of experience + application of skills |
| **Analogy** | Brain **hardware** -- determines the limits of information-processing capacity | Brain **software** -- the outcome of experience acting on fluid intelligence |
| **Example** | Speed with which one can analyze new information | Breadth of vocabulary; how many words one can use |
| **Developmental trajectory** | **Decreases slowly with age** | **Stays level or even increases with age** |
| **Research** | Baltes (1987) confirmed age-related decline | Baltes (1987) confirmed stability/increase |

### Critical Practical Implication
Why can **older, more experienced individuals outperform younger ones** on cognitive tasks (scientific research, chess, etc.)?

Answer: Declines in **fluid intelligence** are **more than offset** by the vast store of knowledge in their **crystallized intelligence**.

### Key Takeaway - Cattell
> "Hardware" (fluid) fades with age, but "software" (crystallized) grows. Experience compensates for processing speed decline.

# PART 3: PASS THEORY OF INTELLIGENCE

## 3.1 Overview (Das, Naglieri, and Kirby, 1994)

Full name: **Planning, Attention, Simultaneous, and Successive (PASS) Theory**

**Motivation:** Clinical observations showed that:
- Brain damage can **spare specific functions** while impairing others
- Individuals with **damaged frontal lobes** can have normal IQs despite frontal lobes being essential for higher cognition
- Some **dyslexic children** have high IQs despite reading difficulties
- These facts challenge the usefulness of a single, one-dimensional g

### The Four Processes

| Process | Brain Location | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | Frontal lobe (prefrontal dorsolateral cortex for reasoning; fronto-orbital cortex for social decisions) | Decision-making about HOW to solve a problem. Involves goal setting, anticipating and monitoring feedback. Guides attention and simultaneous/successive processes. |
| **Attention/Arousal** | Brain stem (arousal); Frontal lobes (mobilization of attentional resources; linked to planning) | Selectively attending to some stimuli while ignoring others. Resisting distractions. Maintaining vigilance. |
| **Simultaneous Processing** | Occipital and parietal lobes (posterior cortex) | Integrates stimuli into groups. Stimuli are perceived as a WHOLE, each piece related to others. |
| **Successive Processing** | Frontal temporal lobe (posterior cortex) | Integrates stimuli in a specific SERIAL order. Example: processing word order to understand English syntax. |

### Input and Output
- **Input:** Can be serial (sequential) or concurrent (parallel)
- **Output:** Three modes (Donald, 1991):
  1. **Movements** -- gross and fine movement
  2. **Mimetics** -- dance, music, theatrical poses, body language
  3. **Language** -- oral, written, graphs, signs

### The Knowledge Base
- All four PASS processes operate in the context of an individual's **knowledge base**
- Knowledge is built from past experience: **tacit** (spontaneous, experiential) and **explicit** (formal, instructed)
- Metaphor from the text: *"The PASS processes are floating on a sea of knowledge; without water they cannot operate and will sink."*

### Practical Applications
- **Cognitive Assessment System (CAS)** (Naglieri & Das, 1997) -- measurement tool
- **PASS Reading Enhancement Program (PREP)** (Das, 1999) -- remediation for reading difficulties
- Program for **arithmetic difficulties** (related to deficiencies in planning)

### Key Takeaway - PASS
> Intelligence is not one thing. It is a system of four distinct brain-based processes operating on a foundation of knowledge. This explains why damage or deficit in ONE area does not collapse ALL intelligence.

# PART 4: CULTURE AND INTELLIGENCE

## 4.1 Vygotsky's Cultural View

- Culture provides a **social context** in which people live, grow, and understand the world
- **Elementary mental functions** (crying, walking, running) are UNIVERSAL
- **Higher mental functions** (problem solving, thinking) are **largely culture-produced**
- Cultures are dynamic: they grow and change, and in the process specify what counts as "successful intellectual development"

## 4.2 Technological Intelligence (Western)
- Technologically advanced societies adopt child-rearing practices that foster:
  - Generalization and abstraction
  - Speed
  - Minimal moves
  - Mental manipulation
- Valued skills: Attention, observation, analysis, performance, speed, achievement orientation
- Standard Western IQ tests measure **exactly these skills**
- NOT highly valued in many Asian and African societies

## 4.3 Integral Intelligence (Indian Tradition)
- **Definition:** A holistic approach where cognitive AND non-cognitive processes are emphasized, with a focus on **connectivity with the social and world environment**
- Contrasts with technological intelligence

### The Sanskrit Concept of "Buddhi"
- Far more pervasive than the Western concept of intelligence
- According to J. P. Das (1994), buddhi includes:
  - Mental effort
  - Determined action
  - Feelings and opinions
  - Cognitive competence (knowledge, discrimination, understanding)
  - Knowledge of one's OWN SELF based on conscience, will, and desire
- Has **affective and motivational** components, not just cognitive

### Four Competencies of Intelligence in Indian Tradition (Srivastava & Misra, 2007)
1. **Cognitive competence** -- sensitivity to context, understanding, discrimination, problem solving, effective communication
2. **Social competence** -- respect for social order, commitment to elders/the young/the needy, concern for others, recognizing others' perspectives
3. **Emotional competence** -- self-regulation and self-monitoring of emotions, honesty, politeness, good conduct, self-evaluation
4. **Entrepreneurial competence** -- commitment, persistence, patience, hard work, vigilance, goal-directed behaviors

### Intelligence in Indian Thought Systems
- Intelligence is seen as a **state, a process, and an entity**
- Its realization depends on one's **own effort, persistence, and motivation** (Srivastava & Misra, 2001, 2007)
- Role of karma and inheritance acknowledged, but expression depends on the **child's own efforts** (Srivastava & Misra, 2001)
- Sri Aurobindo: Ultimate aim of intelligence is **direct cognizance without the mediation of senses** -- i.e., self-awareness, not just sense-based reasoning
- Krishnamurti: "Intelligence is truth, beauty, completeness, and love itself."

# PART 5: MEASURING INTELLIGENCE

## 5.1 Historical Origins: Binet and Simon (1904)

**Context:** Paris school board asked Alfred Binet to develop an objective method to identify children who needed special education.

### Design Principles
Items should be answerable **without special training or study** -- the test should measure ABILITY, not school-acquired knowledge.

Two types of items:
1. So new/unusual that no child has prior exposure
2. So familiar that almost all children would have encountered them

### Sample Tasks
- Follow simple commands or imitate gestures
- Name objects in pictures
- Repeat a 15-word sentence
- Tell how two common objects differ
- Complete sentences

### Publication History
- **1905:** First version -- 30 items. Effective at identifying children needing help.
- **1908:** Revised version -- items grouped by age (3 to 13 years); 6 items per age level. An item was placed at an age level if ~75% of children that age passed it.

---

## 5.2 The Stanford-Binet Test

- Developed by **Lewis Terman** at Stanford University
- Adapted Binet's test for use in the United States
- Revised multiple times
- Yielded a **single IQ score**
- **Stanford-Binet Test:** A widely used individual test of intelligence

---

## 5.3 IQ: Original vs. Modern Definition

### Original IQ Formula (Intelligence Quotient)
```
IQ = (Mental Age / Chronological Age) x 100
```
- Mental age = based on number of items passed; **2 months of "mental age" credit per item passed**
- If Mental Age = Chronological Age: IQ = 100 (average)
- IQ > 100: Intellectually advanced for age
- IQ < 100: Below typical for age

### The Problem with the Original Formula
At some point, **mental growth levels off or stops**, while **chronological age keeps increasing**. Result: IQ scores would artificially DECLINE after the early teen years.

### Modern IQ Definition
**IQ** now simply indicates an **individual's performance on an intelligence test RELATIVE TO persons of the same age** who have taken the same test.
- IQ > 100: Scored higher than the average person in their age group
- IQ < 100: Scored lower than average

---

## 5.4 The Wechsler Scales

Developed by **David Wechsler** to overcome problems with the Stanford-Binet.

### Key Innovation
Included **both verbal AND nonverbal (performance) items**, yielding separate scores for each component.

### Tests in the Wechsler Family
- **WAIS-3 (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale - Revised):** For adults
- **WISC (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children):** For children (now in its 3rd revision, WISC-3)

### WAIS Subtests (Table 11.1)

**Verbal Tests:**
| Subtest | Description |
|---|---|
| Information | General information questions, increasing in difficulty |
| Digit Span | Repeat series of digits read aloud by examiner |
| Vocabulary | Define 35 words |
| Arithmetic | Solve arithmetic problems |
| Comprehension | Answer questions requiring detailed answers |
| Similarities | Indicate in what way two items are alike |

**Performance Tests:**
| Subtest | Description |
|---|---|
| Picture Completion | Indicate what part of each picture is missing |
| Picture Arrangement | Arrange pictures to make a sensible story |
| Block Design | Duplicate designs made with red and white blocks |
| Object Assembly | Solve picture puzzles |
| Digit Symbol | Fill in boxes with coded symbols corresponding to a number |

### Wechsler's Additional Claims
- Wechsler believed subtest **score differences** could diagnose serious psychological disorders (research results: **mixed at best**)
- WISC score patterns sometimes used to identify **learning disabilities**: children scoring high on Picture Completion and Object Assembly but LOW on Arithmetic, Information, and Vocabulary are MORE likely to have learning disabilities (Aiken, 1991) -- though not all findings confirm this

### Current Status
Despite limitations, the Wechsler tests are currently **among the most frequently used** individual tests of intelligence.

---

## 5.5 Aptitude vs. Achievement Tests

| Dimension | Aptitude Tests | Achievement Tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Tests designed to measure the **ability to acquire new information**; used primarily to **predict future performance** | Tests designed to measure **current knowledge** -- the results of previous learning |
| **Examples** | WAIS, WISC, SAT/college entrance exams | Your psychology class exam |
| **Limitation** | Not perfectly distinct -- college entrance exams include vocabulary, which reflects past learning | - |

---

## 5.6 Individual Tests: Measuring the Extremes

### At the Low End: Mental Retardation
**Mental Retardation:** Considerably below-average intellectual functioning combined with varying degrees of difficulty in meeting the demands of everyday life.

Determined by: (1) test scores AND (2) success in activities of daily living

| Category | IQ Range | Functional Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | 40-60 | Can progress to 6th-grade level; can become self-supporting |
| **Moderate** | 35-49 | Can reach 2nd-grade level; can contribute to own support |
| **Severe** | 20-34 | Can learn to speak but cannot benefit from vocational training |
| **Profound** | Under 20 | Requires constant supervision and help |

**Causes:**
- Genetic: **Down Syndrome** -- caused by presence of an **extra chromosome**; usually results in IQ below 50
- Environmental: Inadequate nutrition, drugs/alcohol by expectant mothers, infections, toxic agents, lack of oxygen during birth
- Most cases: **Cannot be readily traced to specific causes**

**Williams Syndrome:** Low IQ but often paired with exceptional musical talent and warm/friendly personality.

### At the High End: Intellectually Gifted
- Defined as IQ of **130 or above**
- Lewis Terman's landmark study (begun **1925**): Followed ~1,500 gifted children
- Findings: As a group, gifted persons earned more degrees, higher occupational status/salaries, better personal/social adjustment, and were healthier at each age than average adults

---

## 5.7 Group Tests of Intelligence

- Individual tests are costly (one-on-one with a trained professional)
- Group tests needed for efficiency
- **WWI trigger:** The US armed forces needed to screen millions of recruits
- **Army Alpha:** For persons who could read (developed ~1917)
- **Army Beta:** For persons who could not read or did not speak English
- Developed by: Psychologists such as **Arthur Otis**
- Successfully used to select candidates for officer training school

---

## 5.8 Requirements of a Good Psychological Test

### Requirement 1: Standardization
- A **large, representative group** must take the test
- Mean and distribution of scores examined
- Well-constructed tests produce a **normal curve (bell curve):** most scores in the middle, fewer at extremes
- Standard IQ tests designed to yield a **mean of 100**
- Allows interpretation of where an individual stands relative to all test-takers

### Requirement 2: Reliability
**Reliability:** The extent to which any measuring device yields the **same result each time** it is applied to the same quantity.

Two types:
1. **Internal Consistency (Split-Half Reliability):** Divide the test into two halves (odd/even items), correlate the scores. If high correlation: test is internally consistent.
   - **Split-Half Reliability:** The correlation between scores on two parts of a test.
2. **Test-Retest Reliability:** Same persons take the test at different times. More similar scores across occasions = higher test-retest reliability.
   - **Test-Retest Reliability:** A measure of the extent to which scores on a test remain stable over time.
   - Intelligence should NOT change dramatically over time (absent major life events), so high test-retest reliability is essential.

### Requirement 3: Validity
**Validity:** The extent to which a test actually **measures what it claims to measure**.

Three types:
1. **Content Validity:** Items are straightforwardly related to the characteristic being measured. (e.g., a test asking about ear length to measure intelligence would have LOW content validity.)
   - **Content Validity:** The extent to which items on a test are related in a straightforward way to the characteristic the test aims to measure.

2. **Criterion-Related Validity:** Scores on the test relate to real-world behaviors (criteria) relevant to the characteristic.
   - **Criterion-Related Validity:** The extent to which scores on a test are related to behaviors (criteria) that are relevant to the characteristics the test purports to measure.
   - Two subtypes:
     a. **Concurrent Validity:** The test predicts relevant behaviors happening RIGHT NOW
     b. **Predictive Validity:** The test predicts relevant behaviors IN THE FUTURE

### Validity Assessment of Standard IQ Tests
- **High validity** for predicting school performance
- **Moderate validity** for professional success in fields like medicine, law, science, engineering (where academic intelligence matters)
- **Low validity** for predicting success in music, executive leadership, politics
- Conclusion: Tests are valid, **but only for certain limited uses**

---

## 5.9 The Cognitive Basis of Intelligence: Processing Speed

### Reaction Time Research
- **Processing speed** (speed of performing simple perceptual/cognitive tasks) correlates with IQ scores
- Correlations between reaction time and IQ: approximately **-0.30 to -0.40** (Neisser et al., 1996; Vernon, 1987; Deary & Stough, 1996; Fry & Hale, 1996)
- Negative correlation: faster reaction = higher IQ

### Inspection Time
**Inspection Time:** The minimum amount of time a particular stimulus must be exposed for individuals to make a judgment about it that meets some preestablished criterion of accuracy.

- Procedure: Individuals shown simple drawings; asked if the longer side is on the left or right. Participants told to take their time and be ACCURATE (not fast).
- Measures: How quickly individuals **intake new visual information**
- Correlation with IQ: **-0.50 or more** (Kranzler & Jensen, 1989) -- much stronger than reaction time
- Theoretical basis: Relates to theories of perception emphasizing that visual information is perceived in discrete samples and combined into judgments

### Cognitive-Based Tests (Beyond Stanford-Binet/Wechsler)
- **Kaufman Assessment Battery for Children**
- **Kaufman Adult Intelligence Test** (Kaufman & Kaufman, 1993)
- **Woodcock-Johnson Test of Cognitive Abilities** (Woodcock & Johnson, 1989) -- attempts to measure both fluid AND crystallized intelligence

---

## 5.10 The Neural Basis of Intelligence

Three lines of converging evidence:

1. **Nerve Conduction Velocity:** Speed with which nerve impulses are conducted in the visual system **correlates significantly** with intelligence measures (e.g., Raven Progressive Matrices) (Reed & Jensen, 1993)

2. **Brain Metabolic Efficiency:** The MORE intelligent people are, the **LESS energy** their brains expend during complex cognitive tasks. Confirmed via **PET brain imaging** (Haier et al., 1993)

3. **Brain Structure:** IQ scores (WAIS) correlate with the **size of certain brain regions**, including the left and right temporal lobes and left and right hippocampus -- even after correcting for overall body size (Andreason et al., 1993)

---

## 5.11 Competency Assessment (From Science to Practice)

**Context:** Standard IQ tests are poor predictors of business world success.

**Competency Assessment:** A method that identifies specific skills distinguishing outstanding performers from average ones.

### Behavioral-Event Interviews (Spencer & Spencer, 1993)
- Outstanding performers AND average performers describe what they said, thought, felt, and did in work-related situations
- Differences between groups are labeled **competencies**

### Key Competencies for Business Success
- Inductive reasoning and analytical thinking
- Strong desire for success
- Flexibility
- Wanting to have an impact on others
- Willingness to take initiative
- Self-confidence
- Good understanding of others (social skills)

**Finding:** Being highly successful requires possessing **several** (not all) of these competencies -- approximately five (Nygren & Ukeritis, 1993)

**McClelland's (1998) study validation:**
- Executives classified as "highly qualified" via behavioral-event interviews were rated as doing a better job by bosses AND received significantly higher bonuses
- ~50% of executives hired the usual way quit within a year; **NONE** hired via competency assessment quit
- Each executive cost $250,000+ to hire/train; competency assessment saved the company **$4 million in one year**

# PART 6: HEREDITY AND ENVIRONMENT IN INTELLIGENCE

## 6.1 Evidence for Heredity

### Family Relationship and IQ
| Relationship | IQ Correlation |
|---|---|
| Identical twins raised TOGETHER | ~+0.90 |
| Brothers and sisters | ~+0.50 |
| Cousins | ~+0.15 |

Pattern: Closer genetic relationship = stronger IQ correlation.

### Adoption Studies (Colorado Adoption Project - Plomin et al., 1997)
- 245 children placed for adoption (average age at placement: 29 days)
- Followed from age 1 to 16
- **Finding:** Correlation between adopted children's IQ and their **biological parents' IQ INCREASED over time**
- **Finding:** Correlation between adopted children's IQ and their **adoptive parents' IQ DECREASED over time**
- Same pattern for specific components and general cognitive ability
- **Caveat:** Adoptive homes were above average in socioeconomic status -- children were NOT exposed to environmental extremes of poverty/deprivation

### Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) Research
- Many genes, each with relatively SMALL effects, probably influence general intelligence (Plomin, 1997; Rutter & Plomin, 1997)
- Chorney et al. (1998): Compared IQ > 160 group vs. average (~100) group
- Finding: High-IQ group more likely to possess a specific gene -- but this gene accounted for only **2% of the variance** in general intelligence

### Identical Twins Reared Apart (Bouchard et al., 1990)
- Same genetic makeup, DIFFERENT environments
- Result: IQs of identical twins reared apart **correlate almost as highly** as identical twins reared together
- Also similar in: physical appearance, dress preferences, mannerisms, personality
- Heritability Estimates (McGue et al., 1993; Plomin et al., 1997):
  - Childhood: ~35%
  - Adulthood: up to ~75%
  - Overall estimate: ~50%

**Why does heritability INCREASE with age?** As individuals grow older, they are **increasingly able to choose or change their environments** to permit expression of their genetically determined tendencies (Neisser et al., 1996). Family constraints diminish; genetic predispositions shape life choices more directly.

**Definition - Heritability:** The proportion of the variance in any trait within a given population that is attributable to genetic factors.

---

## 6.2 Evidence for Environmental Factors

### The Flynn Effect (Flynn, 1987)
- Performance on IQ tests has **risen substantially around the world** at ALL age levels in recent decades
- Average increase: **~3 IQ points per decade worldwide** (larger in some countries)
- Consequence: Tests must be **periodically restandardized** to maintain a mean of 100
- "Average" today = higher actual performance than "average" in the past
- Because genetic shifts don't happen between generations, this MUST reflect environmental change
- Proposed environmental contributors (Flynn, 1999; Williams, 1998):
  - Better nutrition
  - Increased urbanization
  - Advent of television
  - More and better education
  - More cognitively demanding jobs
  - Exposure to computer games
- **Caveat:** Insufficient evidence to conclude any single factor caused the rise (Flynn, 1999)

### Environmental Deprivation and Enrichment
- Deprivation: Absence of key stimulation EARLY in life can reduce intelligence (Gottfried, 1984)
- Enrichment: Removing children from deprived environments and placing them in richer ones **enhances intellectual growth**

**Skeels Study (1938, 1966):**
- 13 children (~2 years old) removed from a stimulation-deprived orphanage
- Placed in care of women with intellectual disabilities in an institution (paradoxically enriching -- more adult contact and stimulation)
- Result: Children's IQs rose dramatically -- initially reported as +29 points; corrected to **+10 to +13 points** (Flynn, 1993)
- Children who REMAINED in the orphanage: IQs dropped by **26 points on average**
- 25 years later: Enriched group all doing well (graduated high school, employed, married). Control group: institutionalized or functioning poorly.

**Indian context:** Studies indicate **negative effects of poverty and deprivation** on intelligence measures, with effects becoming more pronounced with age (**cumulative deficit**) (Misra & Tripathi, 2004; Sinha, Tripathi, & Misra, 1982)

### Effective Environmental Interventions (Ramey & Ramey, 1998)
For maximum impact, interventions should:
1. Begin early and continue for a long time
2. Be intense (home visits several times per week)
3. Deliver learning experiences directly by experts (not via parents)
4. Be broad in scope (multiple enhancement procedures)
5. Be matched to individual children's needs
6. Be accompanied by environmental supports (excellent schools)

### Biological Environmental Factors
- **Malnutrition** (prolonged): Adversely affects IQ (Sigman, 1995)
- **Lead exposure** (in air or lead-based paint): Adversely affects IQ
- **Alcohol and drugs** (prenatal exposure): Adversely affects intelligence

# PART 7: GROUP DIFFERENCES IN INTELLIGENCE TEST SCORES

## 7.1 Environmental Explanation (Mainstream Position)

Most psychologists attribute group differences in IQ test performance **primarily to environmental variables**.

### Test Bias as Evidence
- Tests were standardized largely on **middle-class white persons**
- Interpreting minority group scores using these norms is **not appropriate**
- **Cultural Bias:** The tendency of items on a test of intelligence to require specific cultural experience or knowledge.
- Items familiar to middle-class white children give them an edge
- Finding: Tests ARE about as successful in predicting school performance across all groups (so biased content doesn't make them entirely invalid as predictors)
- Steele & Aronson (1996): Minority children finding items unfamiliar may feel **threatened** by the test, which reduces their scores (a form of **stereotype threat**)

### Culture-Fair Tests
- Designed to include only items ALL groups have been exposed to
- Tend to be **nonverbal** (because minority children may use non-English languages)
- **Raven Progressive Matrices (Raven, 1977):**
  - 60 matrices of varying difficulty, each with a logical pattern/design and a missing part
  - Individual selects the item completing the pattern from several choices
  - Focuses primarily on **fluid intelligence** (concept formation, reasoning, identifying similarities)
  - Less susceptible to cultural bias than other tests
  - BUT: Cannot totally eliminate subtle built-in bias

### Flynn's Two Categories of Environmental Evidence (1999)
1. **Indirect evidence:** Statistical efforts to equate socioeconomic status across groups. Results: MIXED (gap reduced in some studies, persists in others)
2. **Direct evidence:** Actual life changes removing minority persons from disadvantaged environments.
   - Key example: Children of African American soldiers and German women (raised post-WWII in essentially a white European environment). Their IQs were **virtually identical** to white children matched for socioeconomic status (Flynn, 1980). When environmental differences are eliminated, group differences disappear.

---

## 7.2 Genetic Hypothesis (The Bell Curve Controversy)

**The Bell Curve (Herrnstein & Murray, 1994):** Argued for genetic factors in group differences.

**Critical Rebuttals (Sternberg, 1995; Schultze, Kane, & Dickens, 1996):**
- The genetic argument would be valid ONLY if the **environments of the groups being compared were identical** -- they are NOT
- The seed/soil analogy (Figure 11.9):
  - Identical seeds planted in two fields with different soil fertility
  - Within each field: differences due to **genetic factors**
  - Between fields: differences due to **environmental factors** (soil)
  - Therefore, even if intelligence is strongly heritable within groups, differences BETWEEN groups can still be entirely due to environment
- The Flynn Effect itself is analogous: within each generation, intelligence is heritable; but BETWEEN generations, differences must be environmental
- Most researchers conclude: **insufficient evidence** to assign genetic causation to group differences (Neisser, 1997)

---

## 7.3 Gender Differences in Intelligence

Overall: Males and females score **virtually identically** on standard intelligence tests.

### Subtle Component Differences (Small, decreasing over time)

| Component | Advantage |
|---|---|
| Verbal abilities (naming synonyms, verbal fluency) | **Females** higher |
| College achievement tests (literature, spelling, writing) | **Females** higher (Stanley, 1993) |
| Visual-spatial tasks (mental rotation, tracking moving objects) | **Males** slightly higher (Law, Pellegrino, & Hunt, 1993) |
| Object location memory (remembering WHERE objects are) | **Females** better (Silverman & Eals, 1992) |
| Route-finding (finding way back after complex route) | **Males** better |

**Important:** All gender differences are **far smaller than stereotypes suggest**.

### Evolutionary Explanation (Silverman & Eals)
- Before civilization: Humans lived by hunting and gathering
- **Female gatherers** needed to notice edible plants and remember their locations (object location memory)
- **Male hunters** needed to find their way back home after crossing large distances (spatial navigation)
- These different adaptive pressures may explain the current subtle differences

**Caveat:** We cannot run experiments on human evolution, so this explanation cannot be confirmed with certainty.

# PART 8: EMOTIONAL INTELLIGENCE (EQ)

## 8.1 Definition and Context

**Emotional Intelligence (EQ)** (Goleman, 1995, 1998): A cluster of traits or abilities relating to the **emotional side of life** -- including recognizing and managing one's own emotions, being able to motivate oneself and restrain impulses, recognizing and managing others' emotions, and handling interpersonal relationships effectively.

- Goleman's argument: EQ may be **more important** for a happy, productive life than IQ
- Distinct from what IQ tests measure

---

## 8.2 Goleman's 5-Component Model (1995)

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Knowing Our Own Emotions (Self-awareness)** | Awareness of one's feelings and thoughts about them. Without it: cannot make intelligent choices; low expressiveness makes others unable to read you; adverse effect on relationships. |
| **2. Managing Our Emotions (Self-regulation)** | Regulating the nature, intensity, and expression of emotions. Essential for mental health and effective interaction. Persons who cannot control their temper will be avoided and fail socially/professionally. |
| **3. Motivating Ourselves** | Ability to motivate oneself to work long and hard; remaining enthusiastic/optimistic; **delaying gratification** (putting off small immediate rewards for larger later rewards). (Shoda, Mischel, & Peake, 1990) |
| **4. Recognizing and Influencing Others' Emotions (Empathy)** | Accurately reading others' moods and emotions. Allows you to know when to ask for favors. Skilled emotion-generators succeed in sales and politics. |
| **5. Handling Relationships (Social Skills)** | Getting along with others; coordinating group efforts; negotiating solutions; giving feedback without causing anger/resentment; being a team player. Also called **interpersonal intelligence** (Hatch, 1990). |

**Enhancement:** EQ can be developed through training in self-monitoring, self-regulation, communication, and problem solving.

---

## 8.3 Mayer-Caruso-Salovey Model (2000)

Treats EQ as an **ability** (not just a personality trait) to **process information about emotions**.

Four components:
1. **Emotional Perception** -- accurately perceiving emotions in faces, voices, images
2. **Emotional Integration** -- using emotions to facilitate thinking
3. **Emotional Understanding** -- understanding complex emotional blends and transitions
4. **Emotional Management** -- regulating emotions in oneself and others

Measurement tool: **Multifactor Emotional Intelligence Scale**

---

## 8.4 Baron's Personality Model of EQ (1997)

Treats EQ as a **personality trait** -- five components:
1. Intrapersonal
2. Interpersonal
3. Adaptability
4. Stress management
5. Mood

---

## 8.5 Indian Perspective on Emotional Intelligence

- Embedded in **social concerns, virtues, religious traditions, and cultural practices** (Sibia, Srivastava, & Misra, 2003)
- Context-sensitive: emphasizes the role of **significant others** (guru, family, larger society) in shaping EQ
- A person able to manage and regulate emotions is called **Jitendriya**
- Indian EQ can be defined as: **Using emotions to do what is right and fulfill one's duties in life**
- Source text: **Bhagavad Gita** -- addresses how man is caught in emotional crises and how to manage emotions to fulfill duty
- EQ also involves creativity in dealing with challenging conditions (Averill, 1999; Baron & Parker, 2000)

---

# PART 9: CREATIVITY

## 9.1 Definition

**Creativity:** The ability to produce work that is both:
1. **Novel** (original, unexpected)
2. **Appropriate** (it works -- it is useful or meets task constraints) (Lubart, 1994)

- Torrance (1962): Creativity is a **process** of sensing gaps or disturbing/missing elements, forming hypotheses, testing them, communicating results, and possibly modifying and retesting.
- Creativity brings dignity and meaning to life (Raina, 1996)

### Mundane vs. Exceptional Creativity
- **Mundane creativity:** Everyday creativity -- uttering a new sentence, understanding a new concept. Occurs when past knowledge gives clues about where to look for solutions.
- **Exceptional creativity:** Dramatically new ideas -- integrated circuits, Einstein's relativity. Occurs in **"Klondike spaces"** (Perkins, 1997) -- areas where, like gold miners in the Klondike, we don't know even where to begin looking. Requires thinking "outside the box" -- breaking out of "mental ruts."

---

## 9.2 Contrasting Views of Creativity

### Cognitive Approach
- Focuses on basic processes underlying creative thought:
  - Retrieval from memory
  - Association
  - Synthesis
  - Transformation
  - Categorical reduction (mentally assigning objects to basic categories)

### Social Psychology Approach
- Focuses on **personality traits** that make people creative
- **Environmental conditions** that encourage or discourage creativity (Simonton, 1994)

### Motivational Approach
- **Intrinsic motivation** -- creativity sustained from within, without external rewards

### Systems Approach (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996)
Creativity is as much **cultural and social** as psychological.

Three-system model:
- **Person** -- individual practitioner (genetic makeup, talents, experience)
- **Domain** -- cultural system (knowledge, tools, values, practices); transmits existing body of knowledge to persons
- **Field** -- social system (community of practice, gatekeepers: teachers, critics, journal editors, museum curators, etc.); evaluates innovations and retains selected ones

**Key argument:** Creativity is NOT the product of a single individual. It occurs at the **intersection of individuals, domains, and fields**.

Process:
1. Domain transmits knowledge to the Person
2. Person produces innovations
3. Field evaluates and retains (or rejects) those innovations
4. Retained innovations are added back to the Domain

**Gatekeepers:** The field (teachers, critics, journal editors, etc.) determines what counts as creative. In physics, a small number of leading professors certified Einstein as creative -- and hundreds of millions accepted that judgment without understanding the physics.

**Social valuation requirement:** For an idea to be "creative" (not just "original"), it must be **socially valued**. Without social valuation, bizarre and genuinely creative ideas are indistinguishable.

Bhawuk (2003): People strive to excel in areas valued by their culture -- e.g., spirituality is culturally valued in India and continues to produce eminent spiritual gurus.

### Five Steps of the Creative Process (Csikszentmihalyi, 1996)
1. **Preparation** -- becoming immersed in problems that are interesting and arouse curiosity
2. **Incubation** -- ideas churn around below the threshold of consciousness
3. **Insight** -- the "Aha!" moment when the puzzle starts to fall together
4. **Evaluation** -- deciding if the insight is valuable and worth pursuing
5. **Elaboration** -- translating the insight into its final work

---

## 9.3 Indian Perspective on Creativity

- **Indigenous approach** (Misra, Srivastava, & Misra, 2006): Emphasizes cultural variation in conceptualization, manifestation, and societal organization
- Creativity requires both **sensitivity** (a feminine virtue in Indian tradition) and **independence** (a masculine virtue) -- creating a potential cultural conflict for developing creativity
- Gender and creativity: Some studies find **no gender difference** (Das, 1996; Kapoor, 1996; Verma, 1993); others find **boys more creative than girls** (Agarwal & Agarwal, 1999; Hussain & Sinha, 1995)

---

## 9.4 Confluence Approach (The Dominant Modern View)

**Confluence Approach:** An approach suggesting that for creativity to occur, **multiple components must converge** (Amabile, 1983).

### Lubart's (1994) Six Required Resources

| Resource | Description |
|---|---|
| **Intellectual abilities** | See problems in new ways; recognize which ideas are worth pursuing; persuade others of new ideas |
| **Knowledge** | Enough expertise in a field to push it forward |
| **Certain styles of thinking** | Preference for thinking in novel ways; ability to see the big picture (think globally AND locally) |
| **Personality attributes** | Willingness to take risks; tolerance for ambiguity |
| **Intrinsic, task-focused motivation** | Love what you are doing; find intrinsic rewards |
| **A supportive environment** | Environment that encourages creative ideas |

**Key claim (Lubart & Sternberg, 1996):** Only when ALL these conditions are present can a high level of creativity emerge.

---

## 9.5 Research Evidence for Confluence Approach (Lubart & Sternberg, 1995)

Study: 48 adults (18-65 years) asked to produce creative products in 4 domains: **writing, art, advertising, science**

Examples:
- Art: Drawings showing "hope," "rage," "the earth from an insect's point of view"
- Advertising: TV ads for bow ties and the IRS

Measures: Creativity, novelty, perceived effort; fluid intelligence, thinking style, personality

**Three Key Findings:**
1. **Raters agreed significantly** on what was creative -- proving creativity CAN be studied scientifically
2. Creativity in one domain was only **moderately related** to creativity in other domains -- you can be creative in one area but not another
3. **Intellectual ability, thinking style, and personality were all significantly related to creativity**

---

## 9.6 Fostering Creativity (Nickerson, 1999 Summary)

1. Establishing purpose and intention
2. Building basic skills
3. Encouraging domain-specific knowledge acquisition
4. Stimulating and rewarding curiosity and exploration
5. Building motivation, especially internal motivation
6. Encouraging confidence and willingness to take risks
7. Focusing on mastery and self-competition
8. Promoting supportable beliefs about creativity
9. Providing opportunities for choice and discovery
10. Developing self-management (metacognitive skills)
11. Teaching techniques for facilitating creative performance
12. Providing balance

**Torrance (1995):** Enhancing creativity involves: soft thinking, deconstruction/reconstruction, imaging, playing, exploration, dialectical thinking, learning from failure, collaboration/cooperation

### Four Key Abilities Involved in Creativity
1. **Fluency** -- elaboration of many ideas, alternatives, or solutions
2. **Flexibility** -- ability to process information or objects in different ways given the same stimulus
3. **Originality** -- getting away from obvious, commonplace, habit-bound thinking
4. **Elaboration** -- ability to embellish ideas with details

## Exam Tip: The Seed/Soil Analogy (Must Know)

This analogy appears in discussions of group differences and is a favorite exam question:

- **Scenario:** Identical seeds planted in two fields. Field A has poor soil; Field B has good soil.
- **Within each field:** Differences in plant growth are due to **GENETIC FACTORS** (same environment, different seeds -- wait, seeds are identical here, so differences reflect random genetic variation within the seed batch)
- **Between the two fields:** Differences in plant growth are due to **ENVIRONMENTAL FACTORS** (soil fertility)

**Application to IQ:** Even if intelligence is strongly heritable WITHIN each group (individual differences stem from genetics), differences BETWEEN groups can still be entirely due to **environmental differences** (discrimination, poverty, educational access, test bias, etc.). The Bell Curve's error was conflating within-group heritability with between-group heritability.
