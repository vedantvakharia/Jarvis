## WHY Human Resource Management is Important

Human resource management (HRM) policies and practices significantly affect organizational performance. Three key reasons:

1. **Competitive Advantage** - HRM is a significant source of competitive advantage. People-oriented HRM gives organizations an edge by creating superior shareholder value. 71% of CEOs say "human capital" is the key source of sustained economic value.

2. **Organizational Strategy** - HRM is an important part of organizational strategies. Achieving competitive success through people means managers must change how they think about employees and the work relationship - treating them as partners, not costs to be minimized.

3. **Organizational Performance** - The way organizations treat their people significantly impacts organizational performance. Companies on Fortune's "100 Best Companies to Work For" list significantly beat the S&P 500 over 10-year, 5-year, 3-year, and 1-year periods.

**Note:** HR departments don't exist in the majority of businesses (which are small). In smaller firms, HR activities are undertaken by line managers. Even in large corporations, most managers need basic HR skills - knowledge of labor laws, conducting selection interviews, and performance evaluations.

## The HRM Process

Eight activities compose the HRM process (Exhibit 12-1):

- **First three** - Ensure competent employees are identified and selected:
  1. Human Resource Planning
  2. Recruitment / Decruitment
  3. Selection

- **Next two** - Provide employees with up-to-date knowledge and skills:
  4. Orientation
  5. Training

- **Final three** - Retain competent, high-performing employees:
  6. Performance Evaluation
  7. Compensation and Benefits
  8. Career Development

All activities occur within the **External Environment**.

## External Factors That Affect the HRM Process

### The Economy
- Economic cycles directly affect an organization's human resources.
- In downturns: management is forced to reduce staff, cut pay, reorganize work.
- In strong economies and tight labor markets: management must raise wages, improve benefits, seek out retirees, provide in-house training, and make other adaptations to attract and keep qualified people.

### Labor Unions
- A **labor union** is an organization that represents workers and seeks to protect their interests through collective bargaining.
- In unionized organizations, many HRM decisions are dictated by collective bargaining agreements, which usually define: recruitment sources; criteria for hiring, promotions, and layoffs; training eligibility; and disciplinary practices.
- US union participation peaked in the mid-1950s at ~35% of wage and salary workers. Today it is under 11%.
- Unions still hold dominant positions in utilities, transportation, and warehousing, and represent a third of all public sector workers.
- **Indirect effect on non-union workers:** When unions negotiate higher salaries or benefits for members, they influence labor practices for non-union organizations competing against unionized firms.
- Union membership is higher outside the US: Germany ~17%, Canada ~26%, Iceland ~90%+.

### Laws and Rulings
- HRM practices are governed by a country's laws; not following those laws can be costly.
- Decisions regarding hiring, training selection, and compensation must be made without regard to race, sex, religion, age, color, national origin, pregnancy, or disability.
- Exceptions can occur in special circumstances (e.g., a firefighter applicant using a wheelchair can be denied; but the same disability cannot be used to deny a desk job like dispatcher).
- HRM laws and regulations limit managerial discretion; managers must know what they can and cannot do by law.

**Affirmative Action** - Organizational programs that enhance the status of members of protected groups. Organizations refrain from discrimination and actively seek to enhance the status of members from protected groups (minorities, females, disabled).

**Global HRM Laws (brief overview):**
- **Canada:** Canadian Human Rights Act prohibits discrimination on many bases; more decentralized (provincial-level) lawmaking than the US. Language discrimination not prohibited except in Quebec.
- **Mexico:** More unionized than the US. Governed by Mexican Federal Labor Law. Employers have 28 days to evaluate a new employee's performance; after that, employee is granted job security and termination is difficult and expensive. Violations can result in fines and jail sentences.
- **Germany:** Requires **representative participation** - redistributes power, putting labor on more equal footing with management and stockholders. Two forms:
  - **Work councils** - Groups of nominated/elected employees who must be consulted when management makes decisions involving personnel.
  - **Board representatives** - Employees who sit on company's board of directors and represent employees' interests.

### Demography
Workforce trends in the early 21st century are notable for four reasons:
1. Changes in racial and ethnic composition
2. An aging baby boomer generation
3. An expanding cohort of Gen Y workers
4. Skill imbalances

- Women changed the composition of the workforce - from under 30% of jobs in 1950 to 46.9% today.
- By 2024: Hispanics projected to grow from 13% to 19.8% of workforce; blacks steady at ~13%; Asians increase slightly from 5.6% to 6.6%.
- The 55-and-older age group (currently 16.3% of workforce) projected to increase to 24.8% by 2024.
- Gen Y (born ~1978-1994) has been the fastest-growing workforce segment - from 14% to more than 24%.
- Skill imbalances: Changing technology and job complexity created an imbalance between skills organizations need and those available. Two skills in high demand but often lacking: digital literacy and soft skills (communication, critical thinking, working in teams).

## Identifying and Selecting Competent Employees

### Human Resource Planning
**Human resource planning** - The process by which managers ensure they have the right number and kinds of capable people in the right places at the right times.

HR planning entails two steps:
1. Assessing current human resources
2. Meeting future HR needs

**Current Assessment:**
- Managers begin by inventorying data on current employees: name, education, training, prior employment, languages spoken, special capabilities, specialized skills.
- **Job analysis** - A process that defines a job and the behaviors necessary to perform it. Information gathered by: directly observing individuals on the job, interviewing employees individually or in a group, having employees complete questionnaires, recording daily activities in a diary, or having job "experts" identify job characteristics.
- **Job description (position description)** - A written statement describing a job - typically job content, environment, and conditions of employment.
- **Job specification** - A written statement of the minimum qualifications a person must possess to successfully perform a given job. Identifies knowledge, skills, and aptitudes needed.

**Meeting Future HR Needs:**
- Determined by the organization's mission, goals, and strategies.
- Demand for employees results from demand for the organization's products or services.
- After assessing current capabilities and future needs, managers can estimate areas where the organization will be understaffed or overstaffed.

### Recruitment and Decruitment

**Recruitment** - Locating, identifying, and attracting capable applicants.

**Decruitment** - Reducing an organization's workforce (used when HR planning shows a surplus of employees).

**Recruiting Sources (Exhibit 12-3):**

| Source | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Internet | Reaches large numbers; can get immediate feedback | Generates many unqualified candidates |
| Employee referrals | Knowledge about the organization provided by current employee; can generate strong candidates because a good referral reflects on the recommender | May not increase diversity and mix of employees |
| Company website | Wide distribution; can be targeted to specific groups | Generates many unqualified candidates |
| College recruiting | Large centralized body of candidates | Limited to entry-level positions |
| Professional recruiting organizations | Good knowledge of industry challenges and requirements | Little commitment to specific organization |
| Social media | Takes advantage of current employees' connections; particularly effective at reaching younger candidates | Not nearly as effective in reaching older or senior candidates |

**Employee Referrals:**
- Top the list as the best source for finding strong applicants.
- Studies show 30%+ of all hires and 45% of internal hires come through referrals.
- Referrals have the highest applicant-to-hire conversion rate - only 7% apply, but they account for 40% of all hires.
- Referral hires have greater job satisfaction and longer tenures.
- Reason: Current employees know both the job and the person recommended; they feel their reputation is at stake.
- Downside: Since people tend to recommend people like themselves, referrals aren't likely to enhance diversity.

**Social Media:**
- Now the fastest-growing source for job applicants.
- 75%+ of teenagers and young adults say they use Snapchat or Facebook.
- Appeals: expands applicant pool, ease of finding rich profiles, power of online connections.

**Decruitment Options (Exhibit 12-4):**

| Option | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Firing | Permanent involuntary termination |
| Layoffs | Temporary involuntary termination; may last only a few days or extend to years |
| Attrition | Not filling openings created by voluntary resignations or normal retirements |
| Transfers | Moving employees laterally or downward; usually does not reduce costs but can reduce intraorganizational supply-demand imbalances |
| Reduced workweeks | Having employees work fewer hours per week, share jobs, or perform jobs on part-time basis |
| Early retirements | Providing incentives to older/more senior employees for retiring before their normal retirement date |
| Job sharing | Having employees share one full-time position |

### Selection
**Selection** - Screening job applicants to ensure that the most appropriate candidates are hired.

**What is Selection?**
- Involves predicting which applicants will be successful if hired.
- Any selection decision can result in four possible outcomes (Exhibit 12-5):
  - **Correct decisions:** (1) Predicted successful, proved successful on job (successfully accepted); (2) Predicted unsuccessful, not hired (successfully rejected).
  - **Errors:** (1) **Reject errors** - Rejecting candidates who would have performed successfully; (2) **Accept errors** - Accepting those who ultimately perform poorly.
- Reject errors can expose the organization to discrimination charges, especially if applicants from protected groups are disproportionately rejected.
- Accept errors include: cost of training the employee, profits lost due to incompetence, cost of severance, subsequent recruiting and screening costs.

**Validity and Reliability:**
- **Valid selection device** - Characterized by a proven relationship between the selection device and some relevant criterion. Federal laws prohibit using a test score to select employees unless clear evidence shows individuals with high scores outperform those with low scores.
- **Reliable selection device** - Measures the same thing consistently. An individual's score should remain fairly consistent over time (assuming measured characteristics are stable). No selection device can be effective if it's not reliable.

**Types of Selection Tools (Exhibit 12-6):**

*Application Forms:*
- Almost universally used
- Most useful for gathering information
- Can predict job performance but not easy to create one that does

*Written Tests:*
- Must be job-related
- Include intelligence, aptitude, ability, personality, and interest tests
- Are popular (e.g., personality tests, aptitude tests)
- Relatively good predictor for supervisory positions

*Performance-Simulation Tests:*
- Use actual job behaviors
- **Work sample tests** - Hands-on simulations of part or all the work that workers in a job routinely must perform. Each element is matched with a job-performance element to measure applicants' knowledge, skills, and abilities.
- **Assessment centers** - A set of performance-simulation tests specifically designed to evaluate a candidate's managerial potential. Line executives, supervisors, and/or trained psychologists evaluate candidates as they go through one to several days of exercises that simulate real problems they would confront on the job.
- One of the most reliable and valid tools for predicting future job performance.

*Interviews:*
- Almost universally used
- Must know what can and cannot be asked
- Can be useful for managerial positions
- Most are poor predictors of job performance - tend to be unstructured (short, casual, random questions); data gathered is typically biased and only modestly related to future job performance.
- Interviewers tend to favor applicants who share their attitudes, give undue weight to negative information, and allow order of interviews to influence evaluations.
- **Structured interviews** (standardized set of questions for all candidates) can significantly increase reliability.

*Background Investigations:*
- Used for verifying application data - valuable source of information
- Used for verifying reference checks - not a valuable source of information

*Physical Examinations:*
- For jobs with certain physical requirements
- Mostly used for insurance purposes

**Social Media in Selection:**
- Facebook and other social media are being used to gather information and screen potential employees.
- Preliminary evidence suggests this does NOT improve quality of hiring decisions.
- Ratings of applicants' Facebook information found to be unrelated to supervisor ratings of job performance or turnover rates.
- Tends to have adverse impact potential by favoring female and white applicants.

**Realistic Job Previews (RJP):**
- **Realistic job preview (RJP)** - A preview of a job that provides both positive and negative information about the job and the company.
- When recruiters focus only on positives, applicants who only hear good aspects are likely to be disenchanted when reality sets in.
- Problems with inflated information: (1) Mismatched applicants won't self-select out of the process; (2) Builds unrealistic expectations - new employees become dissatisfied and leave; (3) New hires become disillusioned and less committed; (4) Individuals who feel misled may become problem employees.
- Applicants who receive an RJP have more realistic expectations and are better able to cope with frustrating elements.

## Providing Employees with Needed Skills and Knowledge

### Orientation and Socialization

**Orientation** - Introducing a new employee to his or her job and the organization.

Two types:
1. **Work unit orientation** - Familiarizes employees with the goals of the work unit, clarifies how their job contributes to unit goals, includes introduction to new coworkers.
2. **Organization orientation** - Informs new employees about the company's goals, history, philosophy, procedures, and rules. Includes relevant HR policies and possibly a facility tour.

**Socialization** - Helping new employees adapt to the organization's culture. Options (Exhibit 12-7):

| Dimension | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| Formal vs. Informal | Formal: new employee is segregated from the ongoing work setting and differentiated to make newcomer role explicit (e.g., specific orientation programs). Informal: new employees placed directly into job with little or no special attention. |
| Individual vs. Collective | Individual: socialized one-by-one (common in professional offices). Collective: grouped together and processed through identical set of experiences (e.g., military boot camp). |
| Fixed vs. Variable | Fixed: standardized stages of transition with set schedule (e.g., rotational training programs). Variable: no advance notice of transition timetable; advancement when "ready." |
| Serial vs. Random | Serial: role models train and encourage the newcomer (e.g., apprenticeship, mentoring). Random: role models deliberately withheld; employees left on their own. |
| Investiture vs. Divestiture | Investiture: newcomer's qualities and qualifications are confirmed and supported. Divestiture: strips away certain characteristics of the recruit to shape them into proper role. |

- **Institutional practices** (formal, collective, fixed, serial + divestiture) - Replace newcomers' differences with standardized predictable behaviors. Common in police, fire departments.
- **Individual practices** (informal, individual, variable, random + investiture) - Give newcomers an innovative sense of their roles. Common in creative fields (R&D, advertising, filmmaking).

Successful socialization results in an outsider-insider transition that:
- Makes the new employee feel comfortable
- Lowers the likelihood of poor work performance
- Reduces the probability of premature resignation

22% of staff turnover occurs in the first 45 days of employment; cost of losing an employee in the first year is about 3x annual salary.

### Employee Training

Employee training is a key HRM activity. As job demands change, employee skills must change with them.

**Types of Training (Exhibit 12-8):**

| Type | Includes |
|------|----------|
| General | Communication skills, computer systems application and programming, customer service, executive development, management skills and development, personal growth, sales, supervisory skills, technological skills and knowledge |
| Specific | Basic life-work skills, creativity, customer education, diversity/cultural awareness, remedial writing, managing change, leadership, product knowledge, public speaking/presentation skills, safety, ethics, sexual harassment, team building, wellness, and others |

Most popular types include: profession/industry-specific training, management/supervisory skills, mandatory/compliance information (sexual harassment, safety, etc.), and customer service training.

Employee interpersonal skills training (communication, conflict resolution, team building, customer service) is a high priority for many organizations.

**Training Methods (Exhibit 12-9):**

*Traditional Training Methods:*
- **On-the-job** - Employees learn by performing tasks, usually after initial introduction.
- **Job rotation** - Employees work at different jobs in a particular area, getting exposure to a variety of tasks.
- **Mentoring and coaching** - Employees work with an experienced worker who provides information, support, and encouragement; also called apprenticeships in certain industries.
- **Experiential exercises** - Employees participate in role-playing, simulations, or other face-to-face training.
- **Workbooks/manuals** - Employees refer to training workbooks and manuals for information.
- **Classroom lectures** - Employees attend lectures designed to convey specific information.

*Technology-Based Training Methods:*
- **DVDs/CDs/podcasts** - Employees listen to or watch selected media that convey information or demonstrate techniques.
- **Videoconferencing/teleconferencing/satellite TV** - Employees listen to or participate as information is conveyed or techniques demonstrated.
- **E-learning** - Internet-based learning where employees participate in multimedia simulations or other interactive modules.
- **Mobile learning** - Learning delivered via mobile devices.
- **Virtual reality** - Using VR headsets and customized software, employees learn through simulated practice.

Experts believe organizations will increasingly rely on e-learning, mobile applications, and virtual reality.

## Retaining Competent, High-Performing Employees

### Performance Evaluation

**Performance Evaluation/Appraisal Methods:**

*Written Essay:*
- Evaluator writes a description of employee's strengths, weaknesses, past performance, and potential; provides suggestions for improvement.
- (+) Simple to use
- (-) May be better measure of evaluator's writing ability than employee's actual performance

*Critical Incident:*
- Evaluator focuses on critical behaviors that separate effective and ineffective performance.
- (+) Rich examples, behaviorally based
- (-) Time-consuming, lacks quantification

*Graphic Rating Scale:*
- Lists a set of performance factors and an incremental scale; evaluator rates employee on each factor.
- (+) Provides quantitative data, not time-consuming
- (-) Doesn't provide in-depth information on job behavior

*BARS (Behaviorally Anchored Rating Scale):*
- Combines elements from critical incident and graphic rating scale; evaluator uses a rating scale, but items are examples of actual job behaviors.
- (+) Focuses on specific and measurable job behaviors
- (-) Time-consuming, difficult to develop

*Multiperson Comparison:*
- Employees are rated in comparison to others in work group.
- (+) Compares employees with one another
- (-) Difficult with large number of employees, legal concerns

*MBO:*
- Employees are evaluated on how well they accomplish specific goals.
- (+) Focuses on goals, results oriented
- (-) Time-consuming

*360-Degree Appraisal:*
- Utilizes feedback from supervisors, employees, and coworkers.
- (+) Thorough
- (-) Time-consuming

**Electronic Performance Monitoring:**
- **Electronic performance monitoring** - The use of electronic instruments or devices to collect, store, analyze, and report individual or group performance.
- Increasingly replaces supervisor observation, especially in computer-based jobs.
- Widely used in call centers: tracks average call handle time, total calls handled, time on breaks.
- Allows supervisors to do formal reviews with objective data.
- Alerts supervisors almost immediately to performance problems, enabling rapid corrective action.

### Compensation and Benefits

Developing an effective and appropriate compensation system is an important part of the HRM process and a key component in attracting and retaining competent and talented individuals.

**Types of Organizational Compensation:**
- Base wages and salaries
- Wage and salary add-ons
- Incentive payments
- Other benefits and services

**What Determines Pay and Benefits (Exhibit 12-11):**
- Employee's tenure and performance
- Kind of job performed
- Kind of business
- Unionization
- Labor or capital intensive
- Size of company
- Company profitability
- Geographical location
- Management philosophy

**Contemporary Compensation Issues:**

*Skill-Based vs. Variable Pay:*
- **Skill-based pay** - A pay system that rewards employees for the job skills they can demonstrate. An employee's job title doesn't define pay category; skills do.
  - One study found a skill-based pay plan in a manufacturing setting increased plant productivity by 58%, lowered labor cost per part by 16%, and generated favorable quality outcomes.
- **Variable pay** - A pay system in which an individual's compensation is contingent on performance.
  - Nearly 90% of US organizations use some type of variable pay plans.
  - About 50% of Asian and Latin American organizations use variable pay.
- Trend: Make pay systems more flexible and reduce the number of pay levels.

*Bonuses vs. Annual Pay Increases:*
- Some companies propose replacing annual pay raises with one-time bonuses for high-performing individuals.
- Much of the motivating potential of a pay raise is lost when mediocre performers get a 3% raise while top performers get 5%.
- Instead of spreading the wealth, concentrating it on best performers may be more effective.

*Pay Secrecy vs. Transparency:*
- Historically, employers told employees not to share salary information; comparisons were likely to create problems regarding equity, trust/loyalty, motivation, and satisfaction.
- In 2016, an executive order (applying to organizations with government contracts over $10,000) prohibited employers from retaliating against employees for disclosing and discussing compensation information.
- A number of companies began reassessing pay secrecy policies as a result.
- Younger workers are more likely to share salary information: 30% of millennials vs. 8% of baby boomers have shared salaries with coworkers.

*Benefit Options:*
- 57% of employees report benefits being among their top considerations in weighing a job offer.
- 80% said they would prefer additional benefits to a pay raise.
- 87% said the right benefit package would make them less likely to leave.
- Benefits with greatest impact on employee satisfaction (in order): health insurance, vacations and paid time off, retirement plans (pensions and 401k plans).
- Additional benefits should reflect employees' interests, organization's culture, and firm's location.

## Career Development

The final activity in the HRM process.

- In the past 30 years, the trend has been for career development to be the responsibility of employees themselves, not the organization.
- Large companies like IBM and GE once provided formal, multi-year training programs and ongoing career development workshops; such offerings are now far less frequent.
- The current view: it's each individual's responsibility to create "brand YOU."

Two important trends:
1. **Organizations helping employees keep skills current** through supporting "lifetime learning" - many firms offer tuition reimbursement for job-related college classes; companies offer leadership programs to prepare employees for managerial responsibilities.
2. **Companies are expanding and promoting their internship programs** - A three-month summer internship is an excellent way to assess a potential employee's abilities and motivation without the cost and legal issues involved if a permanent employee performs poorly. Interns get the chance to learn by doing and to assess whether the organization is a good fit.

## Contemporary Issues in Managing Human Resources

### Sexual Harassment

**Sexual harassment** - Any unwanted action or activity of a sexual nature that explicitly or implicitly affects an individual's employment, performance, or work environment. Can occur between members of the opposite sex or the same sex.

2017 marked a watershed year - behaviors that were acceptable for decades were replaced by a new standard: "zero tolerance."

**EEOC Definition:** "Unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature constitute sexual harassment when this conduct explicitly or implicitly affects an individual's employment, unreasonably interferes with an individual's work performance, or creates an intimidating, hostile or offensive work environment." Sexual harassment can also include offensive remarks about a person's sex.

For many organizations, the offensive or hostile environment issue is the most problematic.

**Costs:** Almost all Fortune 500 companies in the US have had complaints lodged by employees, and at least a third have been sued. Settlements can range from low thousands to tens of millions.

Sexual harassment is a global issue: 35% of women globally have experienced physical or sexual violence.

Beyond legal costs, sexual harassment creates an unpleasant, often hostile work environment and undermines workers' ability to perform their job.

**Seven-element protection program:**
1. A clear anti-harassment policy
2. An explicit statement of prohibited behaviors
3. A complaint procedure that encourages employees to come forward
4. Protections for complainants and witnesses against retaliation
5. An investigative strategy that protects privacy interests of both alleged victim and accused offender
6. Ongoing management training and employee awareness programs
7. Measures and processes to ensure prompt corrective action to stop ongoing harassment and appropriate disciplinary actions for offenders

### Bullying in the Workplace

**Workplace bullying** - When an individual experiences a number of negative behaviors repeatedly over a period of time that results in physical or mental harm.

Key elements:
- A single episode alone is NOT considered bullying - must be repeated over time
- Includes: verbal abuse, derogatory remarks, humiliation, intimidation, retaliation
- Specific behaviors: having opinions ignored, being shouted at, having gossip/rumors spread about them, being ridiculed in public
- Includes the presence of a **power imbalance** so one party is at a disadvantage or unable to protect/shield themselves

**Scale of the problem:**
- Approximately 29% of the US workforce has been bullied on the job.
- About 4x more prevalent than reports of sexual harassment.

**Effects on employees:** increases anxiety and panic attacks, reduces self-esteem, can create stress-related depression.

**Effects on organizations:** lower employee productivity, increased absences, higher turnover, increased replacement hiring costs.

**Legal status:**
- The US has **no federal laws** against bullying.
- In contrast, Western Europe, Canada, and Australia have specific anti-bullying policies.
- In the US, an affected employee can bring a lawsuit if they can demonstrate the bullying is related to a protected discrimination class (race, color, disability, sexual orientation).

**Management's obligation:**
- Create a workplace free from bullies.
- Requires a multifaceted approach starting with top management's leadership - commitment in both words and actions.
- Supported by: formal policies, codes of conduct, awareness workshops, formal channels for victims to make claims, prompt investigations into incidences, and well-publicized disciplinary actions against abusers.
