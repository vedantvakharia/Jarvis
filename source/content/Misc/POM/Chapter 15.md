## 1. FOCUS AND GOALS OF ORGANIZATIONAL BEHAVIOR (LO15.1)

### Key Definitions

- **Behavior**: The actions of people
- **Organizational Behavior (OB)**: The study of the actions of people at work

### The Iceberg Metaphor

Organizations have two layers:

**Visible aspects (above the surface):**
- Strategies, Objectives
- Policies and Procedures
- Structure, Technology
- Formal Authority, Chain of Command

**Hidden aspects (below the surface):**
- Attitudes, Perceptions
- Group Norms
- Informal Interactions
- Interpersonal and Intergroup Conflicts

OB provides insights into the hidden, informal elements that managers must understand.

### Focus of OB

OB focuses on two major areas:

1. **Individual behavior** - attitudes, personality, perception, learning, motivation (draws from psychology)
2. **Group behavior** - norms, roles, team building, leadership, conflict (draws from sociology and social psychology)

### Goals of OB

The three goals are to:
- **Explain** why employees engage in some behaviors rather than others
- **Predict** how employees will respond to various actions and decisions
- **Influence** how employees behave

### Six Employee Behaviors OB Focuses On

| Behavior | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Employee productivity** | A performance measure of both efficiency and effectiveness |
| **Absenteeism** | The failure to show up for work |
| **Turnover** | The voluntary and involuntary permanent withdrawal from an organization |
| **Organizational citizenship behavior (OCB)** | Discretionary behavior not part of formal job requirements but promotes effective organizational functioning |
| **Job satisfaction** | An employee's general attitude toward his or her job |
| **Counterproductive workplace behavior** | Any intentional employee behavior potentially harmful to the organization or individuals within it |

**Notes on these behaviors:**
- Absenteeism costs US employers ~$3,600/year per hourly worker and ~$2,650 per salaried worker
- Turnover costs range from 16% of an unskilled worker's pay to 213% of a highly trained employee's pay
- OCB examples: helping teammates, volunteering for extended activities, avoiding unnecessary conflicts, making constructive statements
- Counterproductive behavior appears in four ways: deviance, aggression, antisocial behavior, and violence

## 2. ATTITUDES AND JOB PERFORMANCE (LO15.2)

### Definition

**Attitudes**: Evaluative statements - either favorable or unfavorable - concerning objects, people, or events. They reflect how an individual feels about something.

### Three Components of an Attitude

1. **Cognitive component**: The beliefs, opinions, knowledge, or information held by a person
   - Example: "Discrimination is wrong"

2. **Affective component**: The emotional or feeling part of an attitude
   - Example: "I don't like Pat because he discriminates against minorities"

3. **Behavioral component**: An intention to behave in a certain way toward someone or something
   - Example: "I choose to avoid Pat because of my feelings about him"

> Note: In common usage, "attitude" typically refers only to the affective component.

### Four Job-Related Attitudes (Most Relevant to Managers)

#### A. Job Satisfaction

Definition: A person's general attitude toward his or her job. High satisfaction = positive attitude; low satisfaction = negative attitude.

**Satisfaction levels:**
- US satisfaction levels were consistently ~60% from 1972-2006
- Dropped to low 40s in 2010 (attributed to the Great Recession)
- Rose to 51% by 2017
- ~58% of individuals with household income above $75,000 report satisfaction vs ~45% below $75,000 (likely reflects job characteristics, not pay itself)

**Cultural differences:**
- Mexico: 5.88 (highest on a 6-point scale)
- Scandinavia: 5.51-5.72
- USA: 5.46
- France: 4.89
- South Korea: 4.76 (lowest)

**Satisfaction and Performance:**
- The correlation between satisfaction and performance is quite robust
- Higher job satisfaction = better individual performance
- Organizations with more satisfied employees tend to be more effective

**Satisfaction and Absenteeism:**
- Satisfied employees have lower absenteeism (relationship is moderate to weak)
- When alternative jobs are available, dissatisfied employees have high absence rates
- When few alternatives exist, dissatisfied employees show the same low absence rate as satisfied ones
- Liberal sick leave policies encourage all employees to take days off regardless of satisfaction

**Satisfaction and Turnover:**
- Relationship between satisfaction and turnover is stronger than satisfaction-absenteeism relationship
- A pattern of lowered job satisfaction is the best predictor of intent to leave
- Contagion effect: low satisfaction leading to turnover in a work unit affects others in that unit
- Superior performers are less likely to leave regardless of satisfaction (organizations do more to retain them)

**Satisfaction and Customer Satisfaction:**
- For frontline employees: satisfied employees increase customer satisfaction and loyalty
- Satisfied employees are more friendly, upbeat, and responsive
- Lower turnover means customers encounter familiar, experienced service staff

**Satisfaction and OCB:**
- Job satisfaction is moderately correlated with OCB
- Key mechanism: trust - managers reciprocate employees' OCB with trusting behaviors
- Supportive coworker relationships also encourage helpful behaviors

**Satisfaction and Counterproductive Behavior:**
- Dissatisfied employees are likely to steal, socialize excessively, gossip, be absent, or be tardy
- Responses to dissatisfaction are difficult to predict (one person quits, another plays games, another abuses coworkers)
- Better to attack the root cause (dissatisfaction) than to control individual responses

#### B. Job Involvement

**Definition**: The degree to which an employee identifies with his or her job, actively participates in it, and considers job performance important to self-worth.

- High job involvement = strong identification with and care about the work done
- Associated with: fewer absences, lower resignation rates, higher employee engagement

#### C. Organizational Commitment

**Definition**: The degree to which an employee identifies with a particular organization and its goals and wishes to maintain membership in that organization.

- Better indicator of turnover than job satisfaction (more global and enduring)
- Leads to lower absenteeism and lower turnover
- Less important than it once was (employees no longer stay with single organizations for entire careers)

**Perceived organizational support**: Employees' general belief that their organization values their contribution and cares about their well-being.
- High perceived organizational support leads to increased job satisfaction and lower turnover
- The organization's commitment to the employee matters even when employee-to-organization commitment has declined

#### D. Employee Engagement

**Definition**: An employee's involvement with, satisfaction with, and enthusiasm for the work he or she does.

**Measuring engagement (sample questions):**
- Do you have access to resources and opportunities to learn new skills?
- Is your work important and meaningful?
- Are interactions with coworkers and supervisors rewarding?

**Engaged vs. disengaged employees:**
- Highly engaged: passionate, deeply connected to their work
- Disengaged: "checked out," show up but have no energy or passion

**Benefits of highly engaged employees:**
- 2.5x more likely to be top performers than less-engaged coworkers
- Higher retention rates (lower recruiting and training costs)
- Both outcomes contribute to superior financial performance

**Global engagement levels:**
- Worldwide: only 13% of employees describe themselves as engaged
- United States: 32% of employees are engaged

### Attitudes and Consistency

People seek consistency among their attitudes and between their attitudes and behavior. When inconsistency is encountered, individuals resolve it by:
1. Altering the attitudes
2. Altering the behavior
3. Rationalizing the inconsistency

### Cognitive Dissonance Theory

**Cognitive dissonance**: Any incompatibility or inconsistency between attitudes or between behavior and attitudes. Inconsistency is uncomfortable; individuals try to reduce the discomfort.

**Three factors that determine how hard someone tries to reduce dissonance:**
1. The **importance** of the factors creating the dissonance
2. The degree of **influence** the individual believes he or she has over those factors
3. The **rewards** that may be involved in dissonance

**Key implications:**
- Unimportant factors = low pressure to correct inconsistency
- Important factors = individual may change behavior, downplay the behavior, change attitude, or identify compatible factors
- Externally imposed dissonance (e.g., manager's order) = less pressure to change
- High rewards paired with high dissonance = reduced discomfort (individual believes consistency exists)

### Attitude Surveys

**Attitude surveys**: Surveys that elicit responses from employees through questions about how they feel about their jobs, work groups, supervisors, or the organization.

- ~74% of companies use formal, large-scale employee attitude surveys
- Scores are averaged across work groups, departments, divisions, or the whole organization
- Regular surveys alert managers to problems and employees' intentions early

### Implications for Managers (Attitudes)

- Satisfied and committed employees have lower turnover and absenteeism
- Focus on factors that generate positive job attitudes: challenging/interesting work, equitable rewards, supportive working conditions, supportive colleagues
- Survey employees regularly to understand their attitudes
- Understand that employees will try to reduce dissonance - managers can point to external forces or provide significant rewards to reduce pressure

## 3. PERSONALITY (LO15.3)

**Definition**: The unique combination of emotional, thought, and behavioral patterns that affect how a person reacts to situations and interacts with others.

### Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI)

One of the most widely used personality assessment instruments. A 100-question assessment asking how people usually act or feel in different situations. Classifies people across four dimensions:

| Dimension | Poles | Key Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraversion vs. Introversion** | E / I | Outgoing/social/assertive vs. quiet/shy/focused inward |
| **Sensing vs. Intuition** | S / N | Practical/routine-oriented vs. big-picture/novelty-seeking |
| **Thinking vs. Feeling** | T / F | Logic/reason-based vs. values/emotion-based |
| **Judging vs. Perceiving** | J / P | Ordered/decisive/structured vs. flexible/spontaneous/curious |

**16 personality types** result from combining these four dimensions.

**Extraversion (E):**
- Outgoing, social, assertive
- Needs varied, action-oriented environment with others

**Introversion (I):**
- Quiet, shy, focused on understanding
- Prefers quiet, concentrated environment; explores in depth

**Sensing (S):**
- Practical, prefers routine and order
- High need for closure, patient with details, good at precise work
- Dislikes new problems without standard solutions

**Intuition (N):**
- Relies on unconscious processes, sees the big picture
- Likes solving new problems, dislikes repetition, jumps to conclusions
- Impatient with routine details

**Thinking (T):**
- Uses reason and logic; unemotional
- Likes analysis, can make tough calls, may seem hard-hearted

**Feeling (F):**
- Relies on personal values and emotions
- Aware of others' feelings, needs praise, dislikes delivering bad news, sympathetic

**Judging (J):**
- Wants control, ordered/structured world
- Good planner, decisive, purposeful; focuses on completing tasks

**Perceiving (P):**
- Flexible and spontaneous
- Curious, adaptable, tolerant; focuses on starting tasks, postpones decisions

**Validity concerns:**
- Evidence is mixed; most of it stacks up against the MBTI
- More than half the people who retake the test get a different result
- Still very likely to be encountered in organizations - used by over 80% of Fortune 100 companies

### The Big Five Model

Proposes five basic dimensions that underlie all others and encompass most significant variation in human personality. Test scores are stable over time and predict real-life behavior well.

| Trait | Definition | Job Performance Link |
|---|---|---|
| **Extraversion** | Sociable, talkative, assertive, comfortable with others | Better in high social interaction jobs (managerial, sales); predicts leadership emergence; higher job satisfaction |
| **Agreeableness** | Good-natured, cooperative, trusting | Better liked; perform well in interpersonally oriented jobs (customer service) |
| **Conscientiousness** | Reliable, responsible, dependable, persistent, achievement-oriented | **Best overall predictor of job performance**; higher job knowledge; more OCB; less counterproductive behavior; better adaptability |
| **Emotional stability** | Calm, enthusiastic, secure (positive) vs. tense, nervous, insecure (negative) | Most strongly related to life satisfaction, job satisfaction, reduced burnout and intentions to quit; better adaptation to workplace change |
| **Openness to experience** | Wide range of interests, imaginative, artistically sensitive, intellectual | Most creative and innovative; more effective leaders; comfortable with ambiguity; cope better with organizational change; important for training competency |

**Cross-cultural validity:**
- Five factors appear in almost all cross-cultural studies (diverse cultures)
- Differences in emphasis: Chinese use conscientiousness more, agreeableness less than Americans
- Conscientiousness is a valid predictor of performance across jobs in both European and US studies

### The Dark Triad

Three personality traits that together form the Dark Triad (negative/toxic traits, do not always occur together):

1. **Machiavellianism (Mach)**: Named after Niccolo Machiavelli. Pragmatic, maintains emotional distance, believes ends justify means. High-Mach individuals win short-term by manipulating others but lose long-term because they are not well liked.

2. **Narcissism**: Grandiose sense of self-importance, requires excessive admiration, arrogant. Tendency to exploit situations and people, sense of entitlement, lack of empathy, hypersensitive, easily angered. Ironically, more likely to be chosen for leadership positions (perceived as charismatic). Linked to unethical CEO behavior.

3. **Psychopathy** (non-clinical): Lack of concern for others, lack of guilt or remorse when actions cause harm. Antisocial; in leadership positions rely on threats, manipulation, and bullying.

**Why organizations hire Dark Triad individuals:**
- They also embody charm, leadership, assertiveness, and impression management skills
- In condensed periods (like an interview), they are skilled at hiding toxic traits while promoting positive ones

### Additional Personality Traits (Singular Predictors of Organizational Behavior)

#### Locus of Control

- **Internal locus of control**: Believe they control their own destiny
- **External locus of control**: Believe their lives are controlled by outside forces (luck, chance)

Research findings:
- Externals are less satisfied with their jobs, more alienated, and less involved
- Externals are more likely to blame poor performance evaluations on their boss or external events
- Internals explain poor evaluations in terms of their own actions
- National culture influences locus of control (North Americans lean internal; Middle Eastern cultures lean external)

#### Self-Esteem

- Degree to which people like or dislike themselves
- High self-esteem (SE): believe they possess the ability to succeed; take more risks in job selection; choose more unconventional jobs
- Low SE: more susceptible to external influence; seek approval from others; more likely to conform; less likely to take unpopular stands
- High SE is more satisfied with their jobs

#### Self-Monitoring

- Ability to adjust behavior to external, situational factors
- **High self-monitors**: Considerable adaptability; highly sensitive to external cues; can behave differently in different situations; can present contradictions between public persona and private self
- **Low self-monitors**: Cannot adjust behavior; display true dispositions in every situation; high behavioral consistency

Research findings on high self-monitors:
- Pay closer attention to behavior of others; more flexible
- More mobile in careers; receive more promotions (internal and cross-organizational)
- More likely to occupy central positions in an organization
- Can put on different "faces" for different audiences (important for managers playing multiple roles)

#### Risk-Taking

- High-risk-taking managers: take less time to make decisions, use less information
- Low-risk-taking managers: more deliberate, require more information
- Managers in organizations are generally risk averse, but individual differences exist
- Best practice: align risk-taking propensity with specific job demands
  - High risk-taking: better for hedge fund traders (rapid decision making)
  - Low risk-taking: better for auditing accountants

#### Proactive Personality

- Individuals who identify opportunities, show initiative, take action, and persevere until meaningful change occurs
- Organizational benefits: higher job performance, higher job satisfaction, less need for oversight
- More likely to be seen as leaders and change agents; challenge the status quo; entrepreneurial abilities; achieve career success

### Personality-Job Fit (Holland's Theory)

Developed by psychologist John Holland. Satisfaction is highest and turnover lowest when personality and occupation are compatible.

**Six personality types:**

| Type              | Personality Characteristics                                 | Sample Occupations                     |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| **Realistic**     | Shy, genuine, persistent, stable, conforming, practical     | Mechanic, farmer, assembly-line worker |
| **Investigative** | Analytical, original, curious, independent                  | Biologist, economist, mathematician    |
| **Social**        | Sociable, friendly, cooperative, understanding              | Social worker, teacher, counselor      |
| **Conventional**  | Conforming, efficient, practical, unimaginative, inflexible | Accountant, bank teller, file clerk    |
| **Enterprising**  | Self-confident, ambitious, energetic, domineering           | Lawyer, real estate agent, manager     |
| **Artistic**      | Imaginative, disorderly, idealistic, emotional, impractical | Painter, musician, writer              |

**Key points of Holland's theory:**
1. Intrinsic differences in personality exist among individuals
2. The types of jobs vary
3. People in job environments compatible with their personality should be more satisfied and less likely to resign voluntarily

### Emotions and Emotional Intelligence

**Emotions**: Intense feelings directed at someone or something. They are object-specific (reactions to an object).

**Six universal emotions (research-identified):**
1. Anger
2. Fear
3. Sadness
4. Happiness
5. Disgust
6. Surprise

All six surface in the workplace. People differ in the number and intensity of emotions they experience and in their ability to express them.

Some jobs require emotional control (air traffic controllers, ER nurses, trial judges); others require variable emotional intensity (lawyers, sports announcers).

**Emotional Intelligence (EI)**: The ability to notice and to manage emotional cues and information.

**Five dimensions of EI:**

| Dimension           | Definition                                              |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Self-awareness**  | Ability to be aware of what you're feeling              |
| **Self-management** | Ability to manage one's own emotions and impulses       |
| **Self-motivation** | Ability to persist in the face of setbacks and failures |
| **Empathy**         | Ability to sense how others are feeling                 |
| **Social skills**   | Ability to handle the emotions of others                |

**EI and job performance:**
- Positively related to job performance at all levels
- High-performing engineers and Air Force recruiters were distinguished by EI, not academic intelligence
- EI is especially relevant to success in jobs demanding high social interaction

**Controversy:**
- Supporters: intuitive appeal, predicts important behaviors
- Critics: vague, can't be measured, questionable validity

### Implications for Managers (Personality)

About 80% of US private companies use personality tests when recruiting and hiring.

1. **Assess vocational interests in hiring** - matching interests and job requirements increases likelihood of performance and retention
2. **Prioritize conscientiousness** - if looking for one trait associated with superior job performance, hire for conscientiousness
3. **Hire for emotional intelligence** when successful social interaction is a major factor (especially for managerial positions)
4. **Screen out Dark Triad traits** - use intensive interviews, reference checks; the toxic side pollutes work environments; correct hiring mistakes quickly (preferably during probationary period)

## 4. PERCEPTION (LO15.4)

**Perception**: A process by which we give meaning to our environment by organizing and interpreting sensory impressions.

Key insight: none of us sees reality - we interpret what we see and call it reality. We behave according to our perceptions.

### Factors That Influence Perception

Three sources of factors that shape and sometimes distort perception:

**1. The Perceiver**
Personal characteristics heavily influence interpretation:
- Attitudes, personality, motives, interests, experiences, expectations

**2. The Target**
Characteristics of what is being observed:
- Loud people more noticeable than quiet; extremely attractive/unattractive people stand out
- Relationship of target to its background
- Tendency to group close and similar things together

**3. The Context**
The situation in which perception occurs:
- Time, location, light, heat, color, and other situational factors

### Attribution Theory

**Attribution theory**: Explains how we judge people differently depending on what meaning we attribute to a given behavior.

When observing behavior, we determine whether it is:
- **Internally caused**: Under the personal control of the individual
- **Externally caused**: Result of outside factors (situation forces the behavior)

**Three factors used to determine causation:**

| Factor | Question | Yes Result | No Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Distinctiveness** | Does the person behave this way in other situations? | Low distinctiveness -> Internal | High distinctiveness -> External |
| **Consensus** | Do other people behave the same way in similar situations? | High consensus -> External | Low consensus -> Internal |
| **Consistency** | Does the person behave this way consistently? | High consistency -> Internal | Low consistency -> External |

### Attribution Errors and Biases

**Fundamental attribution error**: The tendency to underestimate the influence of external factors and overestimate the influence of internal/personal factors when judging others' behavior.
- Example: A sales manager attributes poor performance to a rep's laziness rather than a competitor's new product

**Self-serving bias**: The tendency for individuals to attribute their own successes to internal factors (ability, effort) while blaming failures on external factors (luck).
- Feedback in performance reviews will be distorted depending on whether it is positive or negative
- Employees with self-serving tendencies are 25% more likely to rate bosses as abusive

**Cultural differences in attribution:**
- Asian managers: more likely to blame institutions or whole organizations when things go wrong
- Western observers: believe individual managers should get blame or praise

### Shortcuts Used in Judging Others

#### 1. Selective Perception
We take in only certain stimuli (cannot assimilate everything). Selections are based on interests, background, experience, and attitudes. We may draw unwarranted conclusions from ambiguous situations.

#### 2. Assumed Similarity ("Like Me" Effect)
The observer's perception of others is influenced more by the observer's own characteristics than by those of the person being observed. Assuming others want what we want.

#### 3. Stereotyping
Judging someone on the basis of our perception of a group they belong to. Stereotypes based on fact may produce accurate judgments, but many are not factual and distort judgment.

#### 4. Halo Effect
Forming a general impression of a person based on a single characteristic (intelligence, sociability, appearance). One trait colors the entire evaluation.

#### 5. Contrast Effect
Our reaction to a person is influenced by other people we have recently encountered. We do not evaluate a person in isolation.

### Implications for Managers (Perception)

- Employees react to perceptions, not to reality
- Whether appraisals are actually objective or wages are actually high matters less than how employees perceive them
- If individuals perceive conditions to be unfair or low, they will behave as if those conditions actually exist
- Pay close attention to how employees perceive both their jobs and management actions

## 5. LEARNING (LO15.5)

**Learning**: Any relatively permanent change in behavior that occurs as a result of experience.

Almost all behavior is learned. To explain, predict, and influence behavior, we need to understand how people learn.

### Operant Conditioning

**Operant conditioning**: A theory of learning that says behavior is a function of its consequences.

Key principles:
- People learn to behave to get something they want or avoid something they don't want
- Behavior is voluntary/learned (not reflexive)
- **Reinforcement strengthens** behavior and increases the likelihood it will be repeated
- **Lack of reinforcement weakens** behavior and lessens the likelihood it will be repeated

Based on B.F. Skinner's research:
- Behavior is determined from without (learned), not from within (reflexive)
- Rewards most effective when they immediately follow the desired response
- Behavior that is not rewarded or is punished is less likely to be repeated

### Social Learning Theory

**Social learning theory**: People can learn through observation and direct experience - not just through direct experience alone.

Much of what we learn comes from watching others (models): parents, teachers, peers, managers, etc.

**Four processes that determine how much influence models have:**

1. **Attentional processes**: People learn from a model when they recognize and pay attention to its critical features. Most influenced by models who are attractive, repeatedly available, important, or similar to themselves.

2. **Retention processes**: How well the individual remembers the model's action, even after the model is no longer available.

3. **Motor reproduction processes**: The watching must become doing - demonstrating the individual can actually perform the modeled activities.

4. **Reinforcement processes**: Individuals are motivated to exhibit modeled behavior if positive incentives or rewards are provided. Reinforced behaviors get more attention, are learned better, and performed more often.

### Shaping: A Managerial Tool

**Shaping behavior**: The process of guiding learning in graduated steps using reinforcement or lack of reinforcement.

When desired behavior is far from current behavior, shaping offers a systematic approach by reinforcing successive steps that move the individual closer to the desired behavior.

**Four ways to shape behavior:**

| Method | Definition | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive reinforcement** | Following a behavior with something pleasant (praise, reward) | Strengthens behavior; increases likelihood of repetition |
| **Negative reinforcement** | Rewarding a response by eliminating or withdrawing something unpleasant | Strengthens behavior; increases likelihood of repetition |
| **Punishment** | Penalizing undesirable behavior | Weakens behavior; eliminates it |
| **Extinction** | Eliminating any reinforcement that maintains a behavior; ignoring it | Weakens behavior; decreases its frequency |

- Positive and negative reinforcement both **strengthen** a desired behavior
- Punishment and extinction both result in learning, but do so by **weakening** an undesired behavior

### Implications for Managers (Learning)

- Employees will learn on the job regardless - the question is whether managers will manage that learning or let it occur haphazardly
- If marginal employees are rewarded with pay raises and promotions, productive employees who see this may change their own behavior (downward)
- If managers want behavior A but reward behavior B, employees will learn behavior B
- Employees will look to managers as models - managers who arrive late, take long lunches, or misuse company resources send a message employees will model
- Align rewards explicitly with the behaviors you want to see
