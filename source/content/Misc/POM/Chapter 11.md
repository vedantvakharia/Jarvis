## Key Definitions

- **Organizational structure**: The formal arrangement of jobs within an organization.
- **Organizational chart**: The visual representation of an organization's structure.
- **Organizational design**: The process of creating or changing an organization's structure. Involves decisions about six key elements.

---

## Six Elements of Organizational Design

### 1. Work Specialization

- Also called **division of labor**.
- Describes the degree to which activities in the organization are divided into separate jobs.
- The core idea: divide a job into several steps, each completed by a separate individual.
- Makes efficient use of the diversity of skills workers have.
- Avoids inefficiency of paying highly skilled workers to do easy tasks.
- **Diseconomies of specialization** (when taken too far): boredom, fatigue, stress, low productivity, poor quality, increased absenteeism, high turnover.
- Today: most managers see it as important for efficiency, but some organizations use minimal specialization and give employees a broad range of tasks.

### 2. Departmentalization

- **Departmentalization**: The basis by which jobs are grouped together.
- Five common forms:
  1. **Functional** - groups jobs by function (e.g., engineering, accounting, manufacturing). Pros: efficiencies from specialization, coordination within area, in-depth specialization. Cons: poor cross-functional communication, limited view of org goals.
  2. **Geographical** - groups jobs by region. Pros: handles regional issues effectively, serves unique geographic markets. Cons: duplication of functions, can feel isolated.
  3. **Product** - groups jobs by product line. Pros: specialization per product, managers become industry experts, closer to customers. Cons: duplication of functions, limited org-wide view.
  4. **Process** - groups jobs on the basis of product or customer flow. Pros: more efficient workflow. Cons: only usable with certain product types.
  5. **Customer** - groups jobs by customer type. Pros: customer needs can be met by specialists. Cons: duplication of functions, limited org-wide view.
- Most large organizations use combinations of multiple types.
- Trends: increasing use of **customer departmentalization** and **teams** (as tasks become more complex and diverse skills are needed).

### 3. Chain of Command

- **Chain of command**: The line of authority extending from upper organizational levels to the lowest levels, clarifying who reports to whom.
- Three related concepts:
  - **Authority**: The rights inherent in a managerial position to tell people what to do and to expect them to do it.
    - **Line authority**: Entitles a manager to directly direct the work of an employee; extends top-to-bottom following the chain of command.
    - **Staff authority**: Positions created to support, assist, and advise those holding line authority (e.g., HR sub-departments).
  - **Responsibility**: The obligation or expectation to perform assigned duties. Authority without responsibility and accountability can create opportunities for abuse.
  - **Unity of command**: The principle that each person should report to only one manager, to avoid conflicting demands.
- **Today's view**: These concepts are far less important now. IT allows employees to access information and communicate across the org without going through the chain of command. Many employees, especially in project-based orgs, report to more than one boss, violating unity of command.

### 4. Span of Control

- **Span of control**: The number of employees a manager can efficiently and effectively manage.
- Traditional view: no more than five or six subordinates.
- Wider spans = more efficient org (fewer levels, fewer managers, lower cost).
- But too wide a span can reduce effectiveness if managers can't lead properly.
- **Today's view**: No magic number. Factors that influence appropriate span:
  - Skills and abilities of manager and employees
  - Characteristics of the work
  - Similarity and complexity of employee tasks
  - Physical proximity of subordinates
  - Degree of standardized procedures in place
  - Sophistication of the organization's information system
  - Strength of organizational culture
  - Manager's preferred style
- Trend: toward **larger spans of control** to speed up decisions, increase flexibility, get closer to customers, empower employees, and reduce costs.
- Average span for middle managers: ~12 employees. For executives: ~7 employees.

### 5. Centralization and Decentralization

- **Centralization**: The degree to which decision making is concentrated at upper levels of the organization.
- **Decentralization**: The degree to which lower-level employees provide input or actually make decisions.
- Not an either-or concept; it is relative - no org is fully centralized or decentralized.

**Factors favoring more centralization:**
- Stable environment
- Lower-level managers less capable/experienced
- Lower-level managers don't want a say
- Decisions are relatively minor
- Organization facing crisis or risk of failure
- Company is small
- Strategy implementation requires top management control

**Factors favoring more decentralization:**
- Complex, uncertain environment
- Lower-level managers are capable and experienced
- Lower-level managers want a voice in decisions
- Decisions are significant
- Corporate culture is open to manager input
- Company is geographically dispersed
- Strategy implementation requires manager involvement and flexibility

- **Employee empowerment**: Giving employees more authority to make decisions; associated with the trend toward decentralization.
- **Today's view**: Managers choose the level that best fits their situation. Trend is toward decentralization.

### 6. Formalization

- **Formalization**: How standardized an organization's jobs are and the extent to which employee behavior is guided by rules and procedures.
- High formalization: explicit job descriptions, numerous rules, clearly defined procedures; little employee discretion.
- Low formalization: employees have more discretion.
- Traditional theorists favored high formalization for efficiency.
- **Today's view**: High formalization works in stable environments; less effective in rapidly changing environments. Many orgs rely less on strict rules.

## Mechanistic vs. Organic Structures

| Mechanistic | Organic |
|---|---|
| High specialization | Cross-functional teams |
| Rigid departmentalization | Cross-hierarchical teams |
| Clear chain of command | Free flow of information |
| Narrow spans of control | Wide spans of control |
| Centralization | Decentralization |
| High formalization | Low formalization |

- **Mechanistic organization**: Rigid and tightly controlled. Natural result of combining all six structural elements in their traditional form. Tall, impersonal structures.
- **Organic organization**: Highly adaptive and flexible. Division of labor exists but jobs are not standardized. Employees are professionals guided by their own standards. Low centralization. No pure mechanistic or organic org exists; trend is toward organic.

## Contingency Factors Affecting Structural Choice

### 1. Strategy and Structure
- Structure should facilitate goal achievement; closely linked to strategy.
- Organic structures suit organizations pursuing innovation.
- Mechanistic structures suit organizations focused on tight cost control.

### 2. Size and Structure
- Large organizations (>2,000 employees) tend toward more specialization, departmentalization, centralization, and rules.
- Beyond ~2,000 employees, size has diminishing influence on structure (already fairly mechanistic).

### 3. Technology and Structure
- Joan Woodward's research identified three technology categories:
  - **Unit production**: Items produced in units or small batches. Low vertical/horizontal differentiation, low formalization. Most effective: organic.
  - **Mass production**: Large-batch manufacturing. Moderate vertical, high horizontal differentiation, high formalization. Most effective: mechanistic.
  - **Process production**: Continuous-process production. High vertical, low horizontal differentiation, low formalization. Most effective: organic.
- General rule: more routine technology = more mechanistic structure; more nonroutine technology = more organic structure.

### 4. Environmental Uncertainty and Structure
- Stable, simple environments: mechanistic designs are more effective.
- Dynamic, complex environments: organic designs are needed for flexibility.
- Mechanistic organizations cannot respond quickly to rapid environmental change; hence the move toward more organic structures.

## Traditional Organizational Design Options

### Simple Structure
- Little departmentalization, wide spans of control, authority centralized in one person, little formalization.
- Strengths: fast, flexible, inexpensive to maintain, clear accountability.
- Weaknesses: not appropriate as org grows; risky reliance on one person.

### Functional Structure
- Groups similar or related occupational specialties together.
- Functional departmentalization applied to the entire organization.
- Strengths: cost-saving economies of scale, minimal duplication, employees grouped with similar-task peers.
- Weaknesses: pursuit of functional goals can overshadow overall org goals; specialists become insulated with little understanding of other units.

### Divisional Structure
- Made up of separate, semi-autonomous business units or divisions.
- Each division has a manager with authority over and responsibility for that division.
- Parent corporation coordinates and controls divisions and provides support services.
- Strengths: results-focused; division managers accountable for their products/services.
- Weaknesses: duplication of activities and resources increases costs and reduces efficiency.

## Organizing for Flexibility in the 21st Century

Traditional designs are often insufficient for today's dynamic, complex environment. Organizations seek to be lean, flexible, and innovative.

### Team Structures
- The entire organization is made up of work teams.
- **Team structure**: No line of managerial authority top-to-bottom; teams design and do work as they see fit, and are held responsible for results.
- In large orgs, team structures complement functional or divisional structures, combining bureaucratic efficiency with team flexibility.
- Effective teams share: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaningful membership, and purpose.

### Matrix Structure
- Assigns specialists from different functional departments to work on projects led by a project manager.
- Creates a **dual chain of command**: employees report to both a functional manager and a project manager.
- Project manager: authority over project-related goals.
- Functional manager: retains authority over promotions, salary, annual reviews.
- Violates unity of command but works when both managers communicate and coordinate regularly.

### Project Structure
- Employees continuously work on projects.
- No formal departments employees return to after a project.
- Employees bring skills to each new project; all work done by teams.
- More flexible than matrix; no departmentalization or rigid hierarchy.
- Managers serve as facilitators, mentors, and coaches.

### Virtual Organization
- Small core of full-time employees; outsources major business functions.
- Also called network or modular organization.
- Highly centralized, little or no departmentalization.
- Advantage: flexibility; allows small players to compete against large orgs; reduces permanent office and hierarchical costs.
- Drawbacks: perpetual flux and reorganization; unclear roles, goals, and responsibilities; increased political behavior.

## Work Arrangement Options

### Telecommuting
- Employees work at home linked to the workplace by computer.
- Employee benefits: no commute, fewer interruptions, better work-life balance.
- Employer benefits: reduced office expenses, access to employees with family/disability constraints.
- Limitations: not applicable to every job; some companies have ended programs due to desire to improve collaboration, speed, and innovation. Harder for employees to stay focused and build relationships without physical co-presence.

### Compressed Work Week
- Employees work longer hours per day but fewer days per week.
- Most common: four 10-hour days (4-40 program), with Friday off.
- Employee benefits: leisure time, fewer commutes, personal flexibility.
- Employer benefits: higher worker satisfaction, fewer absences, less turnover.
- Drawbacks: physically and mentally draining over time; difficulty arranging dependent care; coordinating scheduling and supervision.

### Flextime
- **Flextime**: A scheduling system requiring employees to work a specific number of hours per week but allowing them to vary the hours within limits.
- Typically: a core period (e.g., 9am-3pm) all must work, with flexibility around it (office open 6am-6pm). Some programs allow accumulating extra hours as days off.
- Benefits: increased productivity (reported by 23% of orgs with flextime), reduced tardiness and absenteeism, higher job satisfaction and retention.
- Drawbacks: not applicable to all jobs (e.g., assembly lines, customer-facing service jobs requiring full coverage).

### Job Sharing
- **Job sharing**: The practice of two or more people splitting a full-time job.
- Employer benefits: two-for-one; wider skills/experience; access to skilled workers who only want part-time (retirees, parents).
- Employee benefits: increased motivation and satisfaction; ability to work when otherwise unable.
- Drawbacks: not widely used (only ~18% of US orgs); difficulty finding compatible partners; historically negative perception of partial commitment.

### Contingent Workforce
- Full-time jobs replaced by contingent (temporary/freelance/contract) workers to increase flexibility and decrease costs.
- Growing trend across industries (e.g., 81% of healthcare orgs use contingent workers).
- Issues: legal classification of contingent workers; country-specific legal limits (e.g., Chinese law limits use to 6 months instead of full-time).
- Outlook: reliance on contingent workforce will increase due to dynamic environments and need for flexibility; but full displacement of traditional jobs likely a decade or more away (in 2017, only 10% of American workers were in temporary or freelance jobs).
