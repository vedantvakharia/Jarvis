## Part 1: Groups and Group Development (LO 13.1)

### What Is a Group?

A **group** = two or more interacting and interdependent individuals who come together to achieve specific goals.

Groups can be:
- **Formal groups** - defined by the organization's structure; have designated work assignments and specific tasks directed at accomplishing organizational goals
- **Informal groups** - social groups; occur naturally in the workplace; tend to form around friendships and common interests

#### Types of Formal Work Groups

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Command groups | Determined by the org chart; individuals who report directly to a given manager |
| Task groups | Brought together to complete a specific job task; often temporary |
| Cross-functional teams | Bring together knowledge/skills from various work areas; members may be trained to do each other's jobs |
| Self-managed teams | Essentially independent; take on traditional managerial responsibilities like hiring, planning, scheduling, evaluating |

---

### Stages of Group Development

#### Stage 1 - Forming
- Two phases: (1) people join the group, (2) defining the group's purpose, structure, and leadership
- Significant uncertainty; members "test the waters" to determine acceptable behavior
- Stage complete when members begin to think of themselves as part of a group

#### Stage 2 - Storming
- Characterized by intragroup conflict
- Conflict over who controls the group and what the group needs to be doing
- A relatively clear leadership hierarchy and agreement on the group's direction emerge by end of stage

#### Stage 3 - Norming
- Close relationships develop; group becomes cohesive
- Strong sense of group identity and camaraderie
- Stage complete when group structure solidifies and members share a common set of expectations (norms)

#### Stage 4 - Performing
- Group structure is in place and accepted
- Energy shifts from getting to know each other to working on the task
- This is the **final stage for permanent work groups**

#### Stage 5 - Adjourning
- Final stage for **temporary groups** only
- Group prepares to disband
- Focus shifts to wrapping up activities rather than task performance
- Members react differently: some upbeat, some sad over loss of camaraderie

#### Important Caveats About the Stage Model
- Groups do NOT always progress sequentially through stages
- Groups can storm and perform simultaneously
- Groups can regress to previous stages
- High conflict (storming) can sometimes produce high performance
- Think of it as a general framework, not a rigid prescription
- Managers need to know what stage a group is in to anticipate likely problems

## Part 2: Work Group Performance and Satisfaction (LO 13.2)

The **Group Performance/Satisfaction Model** identifies four major factors:

1. External Conditions Imposed on the Group
2. Group Member Resources
3. Group Structure
4. Group Processes
5. Group Tasks (moderates the relationship between processes and outcomes)

---

### Factor 1: External Conditions Imposed on the Group

Work groups are affected by external conditions such as:
- Organization's strategy
- Authority relationships
- Formal rules and regulations
- Availability of resources
- Employee selection criteria
- Performance evaluation system and culture
- General physical layout of the workspace

These conditions are largely outside the group's control but directly shape behavior and output.

---

### Factor 2: Group Member Resources

A group's performance potential depends on what individuals bring:
- Knowledge
- Abilities
- Skills
- Personality traits

#### Interpersonal Skills That Matter Most
- Conflict management and resolution
- Collaborative problem solving
- Communication

These consistently emerge as important for high group performance.

#### Personality and Group Performance
- **Positive traits** (sociability, self-reliance, independence) - positively related to group productivity and morale
- **Negative traits** (authoritarianism, dominance, unconventionality) - negatively related to group productivity and morale

---

### Factor 3: Group Structure

Group structure defines roles, norms, conformity, status systems, group size, group cohesiveness, and leadership.

#### A. Roles

A **role** = behavior patterns expected of someone occupying a given position in a social unit.

Two general role orientations in groups:
- **Task accomplishment role** - keeping the group focused on getting work done
- **Group member satisfaction role** - ensuring members have the opportunity to offer ideas and feel satisfied

Both types of roles are necessary for a group to function effectively and efficiently.

---

#### B. Norms

**Norms** = standards or expectations that are accepted and shared by a group's members.

Norms dictate things like:
- Work output levels
- Absenteeism
- Promptness
- Amount of socializing on the job

##### Common Organizational Norms:

1. **Effort and performance norms** - most widespread; provide explicit cues on how hard to work, level of output, when to look busy, when it's acceptable to goof off. So powerful they can override individual skill and motivation.

2. **Dress norms** - dictate what is acceptable to wear to work; those who deviate face subtle pressure to conform.

3. **Loyalty norms** - influence whether individuals work late, work weekends, or relocate.

---

#### C. Conformity

- Individuals want to be accepted by groups, making them susceptible to pressure to conform
- **Asch's conformity experiments**: Groups of 7-8 people asked to compare line lengths. When confederates gave obviously wrong answers, the unsuspecting subject conformed over 1/3 of the time.
- Findings are still valid today
- Conformity is a powerful force; people prefer to agree rather than be disruptive, even when being disruptive would improve effectiveness

##### Groupthink
- **Groupthink** = when a group exerts extensive pressure on an individual to align their opinion with others' opinions
- Occurs when group members hold a positive group image they want to protect and perceive a collective threat to that image
- Can lead to catastrophic outcomes (e.g., NASA Challenger disaster - "go for launch" mentality overrode expressed safety concerns)

---

#### D. Status Systems

**Status** = a prestige grading, position, or rank within a group.

- Found in all groups throughout history
- Can be a significant motivator with behavioral consequences
- Individuals who perceive a disparity between their perceived status and what others perceive are particularly affected

##### Types of Status:
- **Informal status** - conferred by characteristics like education, age, skill, or experience; anything the group evaluates as having status value
- **Formal status** - conferred by the organization through titles, office size, pay, perks, etc.

##### Status Congruence
- It is important that the formal status system is **congruent** - consistency between perceived ranking of an individual and the status symbols given by the organization
- **Status incongruence** = when status symbols don't match perceived rank (e.g., supervisor earns less than subordinates, desirable office occupied by low-ranking person)
- Effects of incongruence: employees become stressed, may question manager authority, may not be motivated by promotion opportunities

---

#### E. Group Size

Group size affects performance depending on the task:

| Group Size | Best For |
|---|---|
| Large (12+ members) | Fact-finding; getting diverse input |
| Small (5-7 members) | Doing something productive with facts; problem solving |

- Small groups are **faster** at completing tasks
- Large groups **get better results** in problem solving

##### Social Loafing
- **Social loafing** = tendency for individuals to expend less effort when working collectively than when working individually
- Causes: (1) people believe others aren't doing their fair share, (2) relationship between individual input and group output is unclear
- Creates "free riders" who coast on the group's efforts
- **Managerial implication**: managers must identify individual efforts, not just group output. Failure to do so leads to declining productivity and satisfaction.

---

#### F. Group Cohesiveness

**Group cohesiveness** = degree to which group members are attracted to one another and share the group's goals.

##### Cohesiveness and Productivity Matrix:

| | High Cohesiveness | Low Cohesiveness |
|---|---|---|
| **High goal alignment** | Strong increase in productivity | Moderate increase in productivity |
| **Low goal alignment** | Decrease in productivity | No significant effect on productivity |

Key insight: Cohesiveness amplifies the direction of the group's goals - good goals + high cohesion = excellent performance; bad goals + high cohesion = actively harmful to performance.

---

### Factor 4: Group Processes

Processes are important because they influence performance and satisfaction either positively or negatively.

- **Positive process example**: Synergy - group generates far more ideas together than members could individually
- **Negative process examples**: Social loafing, high conflict, poor communication

Two key group processes covered:

#### A. Group Decision Making

##### Advantages of Group Decisions:
1. Generate more complete information and knowledge
2. Bring diversity of experiences and perspectives
3. Generate more diverse alternatives
4. Increase acceptance of a solution (members reluctant to undermine what they helped create)
5. Increase legitimacy (perceived as more legitimate than one-person decisions)

##### Disadvantages of Group Decisions:
1. Almost always take more time than individual decisions
2. A dominant vocal minority can heavily influence the outcome
3. Groupthink can undermine critical thinking
4. Responsibility is shared but ambiguous for any single member

##### When to Use Group vs. Individual Decisions:

| Criterion | Better Choice |
|---|---|
| Accuracy, creativity, acceptance | Group decision |
| Speed, efficiency | Individual decision |

##### Optimal Group Size for Decision Making:
- Groups of **5** are most effective; groups of **7** to a lesser extent
- Odd number helps avoid deadlocks
- Large enough to shift roles; small enough for quieter members to participate

##### Techniques for Creative Group Decision Making:

| Technique | Description |
|---|---|
| **Brainstorming** | Idea-generating process that encourages alternatives while withholding criticism |
| **Nominal Group Technique (NGT)** | Members independently write ideas, then present one at a time with no discussion until all ideas are presented |
| **Electronic Meetings** | Decision-making groups that interact via linked computers |

---

#### B. Conflict Management

**Conflict** = perceived incompatible differences resulting in some form of interference or opposition. Whether the differences are real is irrelevant - if people perceive differences exist, there is conflict.

##### Three Views of Conflict:

| View | Core Belief |
|---|---|
| **Traditional view** | All conflict is bad and must be avoided; conflict indicates a problem |
| **Human relations view** | Conflict is natural and inevitable; need not be negative; can be a positive force |
| **Interactionist view** (most recent) | Some conflict is absolutely necessary for a group to perform effectively |

##### Types of Conflict:

- **Functional conflicts** - constructive; support group goals and improve performance
- **Dysfunctional conflicts** - destructive; prevent achieving goals

##### Three Types by Subject Matter:

| Type | Focus | Functional or Dysfunctional? |
|---|---|---|
| **Task conflict** | Content and goals of the work | Low-to-moderate levels = functional; stimulates discussion, innovation |
| **Process conflict** | How the work gets done | Must be minimal to be productive; intense = dysfunctional |
| **Relationship conflict** | Interpersonal relationships | Almost always dysfunctional; increases hostility, decreases understanding |

##### Conflict and Performance Relationship (Exhibit 13-7):
- **Low/no conflict (Situation A)**: Dysfunctional - group is apathetic, stagnant, unresponsive to change, lacks new ideas -> LOW performance
- **Optimal conflict (Situation B)**: Functional - group is viable, self-critical, innovative -> HIGH performance
- **High conflict (Situation C)**: Dysfunctional - group is disruptive, chaotic, uncooperative -> LOW performance

---

### Factor 5: Group Tasks

The complexity and interdependence of tasks moderate the impact of group processes on performance.

| Task Type | Characteristics | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple tasks** | Routine and standardized | Standard procedures suffice; less need for group discussion |
| **Complex tasks** | Novel or nonroutine | Group benefits more from discussion of alternative work methods |

- Higher task interdependence = members must interact more = communication and conflict management become more critical
- Group processes matter most when tasks are **complex AND interdependent**

## Part 3: Turning Groups into Effective Teams (LO 13.3)

### Why Teams?

- Work increasingly done in teams across organizations
- Teams typically outperform individuals when tasks require multiple skills, judgment, and experience
- Teams are more flexible and responsive to changing events than traditional departments
- Teams can quickly assemble, deploy, refocus, and disband

---

### The Difference Between Groups and Teams

| Dimension | Work Groups | Work Teams |
|---|---|---|
| Leadership | One leader clearly in charge | Leadership role is shared |
| Accountability | Accountable only to self | Accountable to self AND team |
| Purpose | Same as broader organizational purpose | Team creates its own specific purpose |
| Work style | Done individually | Done collectively |
| Meetings | Efficient; no collaboration or open-ended discussion | Open-ended discussion and collaborative problem solving |
| Performance measurement | Measured indirectly by influence on others | Measured directly by evaluating collective work output |
| Work decisions | Decided by group leader, delegated to individuals | Decided upon and done together |
| Adaptability | Relatively fixed | Can be quickly assembled, deployed, refocused, disbanded |

**Work teams** = groups whose members work intensely on a specific, common goal using their positive synergy, individual and mutual accountability, and complementary skills.

---

### Types of Work Teams

#### 1. Problem-Solving Teams
- Members from the **same department or functional area**
- Share ideas or suggestions on how to improve work processes and methods
- Rarely given authority to implement suggested actions
- First type of work team to become popular

#### 2. Self-Managed Work Teams
- Formal group of employees who operate **without a manager**
- Responsible for a complete work process or segment
- Handle: scheduling work, assigning tasks, controlling pace, making operating decisions, taking action on problems
- Assume traditional supervisory tasks (planning, scheduling, evaluating)

#### 3. Cross-Functional Teams
- Composed of individuals from **various functional specialties**
- Bring together diverse knowledge and skills
- Apply expertise from multiple domains to solve complex problems
- Very common in both corporate and health care settings

#### 4. Virtual Teams
- Use **computer technology** to link physically dispersed members
- Collaborate via wide-area networks, corporate social media, videoconferencing, email
- Members can be anywhere in the world
- Enables assembling teams with specialty expertise from multiple locations

---

### Creating Effective Work Teams

Research identifies 9 characteristics associated with effective teams (Exhibit 13-9):

#### 1. Clear Goals
- High-performance teams have a clear understanding of the goal to be achieved
- Members are committed to the team's goals
- Everyone knows what they are expected to accomplish and how they need to work together

#### 2. Relevant Skills
- Composed of competent individuals with necessary **technical AND interpersonal** skills
- Technical competence alone is not sufficient - interpersonal skills to work well as a team member are equally critical

#### 3. Mutual Trust
- Members believe in each other's **ability, character, and integrity**
- Without mutual trust, honest guidance and open feedback become impossible
- Foundation of all effective team relationships

#### 4. Unified Commitment
- Dedication to the team's goals
- Willingness to expend extraordinary amounts of energy to achieve them
- Intense loyalty to the team
- Willingness to do whatever it takes to help the team succeed

#### 5. Good Communication
- Members convey messages - verbally and nonverbally - in ways that are readily and clearly understood
- Feedback helps guide members and correct misunderstandings
- High-performing teams can quickly and efficiently share ideas and feelings

#### 6. Negotiating Skills
- Teams are continually making adjustments about who does what
- Requires flexibility and willingness to confront and reconcile differences
- Problems and relationships regularly change - members must adapt

#### 7. Appropriate Leadership
- Effective leaders clarify goals, demonstrate that change is possible, increase team self-confidence, help members realize their potential
- Increasingly, effective team leaders act as **coaches and facilitators** - they guide and support but do not control

#### 8. Internal Support
- Sound infrastructure: proper training, clear measurement systems, incentive program that recognizes team activities, supportive human resource systems
- Infrastructure should reinforce behaviors that lead to high performance

#### 9. External Support
- Managers provide the team with resources needed to get the job done
- External climate must be supportive of the team's work

### Handling Difficult Coworkers (Workplace Confidential)

#### Step 1: Depersonalize
- Recognize it's the **behavior** getting to you, not the person
- Do not make it personal
- Understanding the behavior helps identify the source and potential solutions

#### Step 2: Self-Examine
- Ask: to what degree might I be contributing to the problem?
- Remove controllable factors you may be bringing to the situation (e.g., moodiness, being too demanding)

#### Step 3: Diagnose
- Identify specifically what bothers you
- Attempt to see things from the coworker's perspective
- Assess whether it's a **clash of styles** vs. a **toxic worker** - this distinction matters

##### Style Clashes vs. Toxic Workers:

- **Clash of styles** - different approaches to promptness, deadlines, communication, change, stress, mistakes. No objective right/wrong; just different ways of seeing things.
- **Toxic worker** - something inside (anger, distrust) that infects those around them; harder to resolve

#### Strategies by Difficult Type:

| Type | Strategy |
|---|---|
| **Aggressive types** | Stand up for yourself; give them time to run down; speak from your own point of view; avoid head-on fights; be ready to be friendly |
| **Complainers** | Listen attentively; acknowledge concerns; interrupt the complaint loop; don't agree but do acknowledge; state facts without apology; redirect to problem solving |
| **Silent/nonresponsive** | Ask open-ended questions; use the friendly silent stare; don't fill silence for them; comment on what's happening to ease tension |
| **Know-it-all experts** | Be well-prepared; listen and acknowledge; question firmly without confronting; avoid becoming a counterexpert; channel their energy positively |

#### The Toxic Coworker - Dark Triad:

Three traits characterizing toxic workers:

1. **Psychopathy** - personality disorder; lack of remorse and empathy; emotionally cold and disconnected; most live normal lives
2. **Machiavellianism** - master manipulators; cunning and duplicitous; often bosses/upper managers because they are skilled at impressing others; use "soft" tactics (compliments, reason)
3. **Narcissism** - highly self-centered; egotistical; use "soft" tactics alongside Machiavellians; psychopaths use "hard" tactics (assertiveness, direct manipulation)

#### Dealing with Toxic Coworkers:
- **Best option**: physically distance yourself - keep a safe distance to avoid being pulled into the toxicity (toxic behavior is contagious)
- **If physical distance not possible**: distance yourself mentally and emotionally by setting firm boundaries - communicate what you will and won't accept; maintain those boundaries through repetition; make consequences clear
- **Escalation**: take succinct, professional complaints to superiors; clearly identify which workplace rules are being broken and how it affects work performance
