## Author: Aravind Adiga (b. 1974)

Aravind Adiga was born in Madras (Chennai) in 1974 and grew up in Mangalore. His family later immigrated to Australia, where he completed his schooling in Sydney.

**Education and Career:**

- Adiga studied English Literature at Columbia University, New York, and later at Magdalene College, Oxford.
- He worked as a journalist and literary reviewer for various publications, including the Financial Times, The Independent, The Sunday Times, and Time.
- He eventually gave up his job as a journalist, correspondent, and reviewer to dedicate himself to writing fiction.
- Adiga’s debut novel, _The White Tiger_, was published in 2008.
- He won the Man Booker Prize in 2008 for _The White Tiger_, joining the ranks of other Indian-born authors who have received the award.

### High-Level Overview of _The White Tiger_ (2008)

The White Tiger is a darkly satirical portrait of modern India that engages with the life of Balram Halwai.

**Structure and Narrative:**
- The novel is an epistolary novel, told from the first-person perspective of its protagonist, Balram Halwai.
- It is framed as a series of letters (a monologue) written by Balram to Wen Jiabao, the Chinese Premier or Prime Minister from 2003 to 2013. Balram is writing specifically because he heard a lady on All India Radio announce that "Premier Jiabao is coming to Bangalore next week".
- Balram, an entrepreneur in Bangalore, begins his correspondence by offering to tell Mr. Jiabao the "truth about Bangalore" and the real story of India.
- The narrative critiques the "moral and saintly" image of India presented by its leaders, which Balram dismisses as a "joke". He exposes pervasive corruption, poverty, and servitude.

**Plot and Themes:**
- Balram Halwai is born into a poor Halwai (sweet-maker) caste family in Laxmangarh, a village marked by poverty and landlord oppression.
- Due to his rare intelligence and ability to read and answer questions proficiently, a school inspector nicknamed him "The White Tiger"—a creature that others notice but can also be incredibly dangerous.
- After his education is cut short, Balram eventually learns to drive and secures a job as a chauffeur for Mr. Ashok, the foreign-educated son of a powerful landlord.
- Balram's journey takes him from rural Laxmangarh to Dhanbad, Delhi (representing moral decay), and finally to Bangalore (symbolizing opportunity). This journey offers a 'substratum of Indian reality'.
- In Delhi, Balram witnesses political corruption firsthand through his master's involvement in bribing politicians.
- The novel uses the Rooster Coop metaphor to describe the psychological entrapment of India’s poor, who accept servitude due to family loyalty. Balram realizes he can only break free by taking "drastic action".
- Balram commits a brutal act of "entrepreneurship" (murder) by killing Mr. Ashok and stealing 700,000 rupees. He uses this stolen money to bribe police officers and establish his own successful taxi service in Bangalore, "White Tiger Drivers".
- The novel exposes class struggle and inequality, suggesting that modern India possesses only two castes: "Men with Big Bellies" (the rich) and "Men with Small Bellies" (the poor).
- Balram changes his name to "Ashok Sharma" to erase his past, mirroring the corrupt practices of his former employers and accepting that his family was likely murdered in retaliation—a price he accepts for his freedom.

### Aravind Adiga's Intent: The "Worm's Eye View"

Adiga’s intent for writing _The White Tiger_ was to offer a perspective of postmillennial India that is often ignored by the elite. It emphasizes that literature should be a portrayal of the reality of society, not just a product of imagination. He aimed to highlight the brutal realities faced by the lower classes.

**The "Noise Never Gets Recorded":**
- Adiga spoke about capturing the undercurrent of Indian life, stating: "There’s a continuous murmur or growl beneath middle-class life in India and this noise never gets recorded".
- He explained that in preparing for the novel, he spent time "loitering about train stations, or bus stands, or servant’s quarters and slums," where he listened and talked to the people around him.
- Adiga stressed that this "reality for a lot of Indian people" must be written about.

**Highlighting Brutal Injustice:**
- His intent was to use literature to advocate for societal improvement: "at a time when India is going through great changes, it is important that writers like me try to highlight the brutal injustices of society".
- He linked his work to the tradition of 19th-century European writers, noting that writers like Flaubert, Balzac, and Dickens highlighted societal issues, which resulted in England and France becoming "better societies". This suggests the novel serves as a satirical critique of Indian society intended to expose corruption and inequality.

Adiga's effort to record the "noise never gets recorded" is akin to a geological survey, where official maps only show the mountain peaks of success (the "Light"), but the writer's duty is to drill down to the substratum—the hidden, complex, and often harsh reality—that supports the entire structure.

## The Primary Text Excerpt (Balram's First Letter)

**Who he is writing to (Wen Jiabao) -** Balram is writing to His Excellency Wen Jiabao, whose address is listed as The Premier's Office, Beijing, Capital of the Freedom-loving Nation of China. Balram refers to him as "Mr. Premier, Sir". He decided to dictate the letter after the lady on All India Radio announced that Premier Jiabao was coming to Bangalore next week.

**His self-description ('The White Tiger,' 'Entrepreneur') -** Balram's letter is sent from the desk of "The White Tiger," A Thinking Man, And an Entrepreneur. He considers himself one of your kind (a great man). He describes himself as a self-taught entrepreneur, which he claims "is the best kind there is". He admits he may be "somewhat lacking" in formal education and never finished school. He claims to know by heart the works of the "four greatest poets of all time": **Rumi, Iqbal, Mirza Ghalib**, and a fourth fellow whose name he forgets. He also mentions learning about Chinese history from a book he found on a pavement in Old Delhi titled _Exciting Tales of the Exotic East_. Balram believes the future of the world lies with the yellow man and the brown man because the erstwhile master, the white-skinned man, has wasted himself through buggery, cell phone usage, and drug abuse. In expressing his admiration for China, Balram notes that "Only three nations have never let themselves be ruled by foreigners: China, Afghanistan, and Abyssinia". This explains _why_ he respects the Premier so much. Balram warns the Premier that when he visits Bangalore, boys at traffic lights will try to sell him bootlegged American business books like _Ten Secrets of Business Success!_ . Balram advises him not to waste money on them because "They're so yesterday. I am tomorrow" .

**His location (Bangalore) -** He is living in the world's center of Technology and Outsourcing at Electronics City Phase 1 (just off Hosur Main Road) in Bangalore, India.

**His stated reason for writing (to tell the 'truth about Bangalore' and entrepreneurs) -** Balram was shocked when the radio lady announced that Mr. Jiabao is on a mission to know the truth about Bangalore. Balram claims that if anyone knows the truth about Bangalore, it would be him. He decided to dictate a letter to the Premier to tell him the truth about Bangalore by telling his life's story. He states that when the Premier hears the story of how Balram got to Bangalore and became one of its most successful businessmen (though probably least known), he will know "everything there is to know about how entrepreneurship is born, nurtured, and developed" in the twenty-first century.

**The English phrase he learned from Pinky Madam:** Balram's ex-employer the late Mr. Ashok's ex-wife, Pinky Madam, taught him one phrase that can be said only in English. The phrase, which he says out loud whenever great men like the Premier visit India, is: **What a fucking joke**. Balram says this phrase when he reflects that Indian leaders will greet the Premier and tell him how "moral and saintly India is" and present him with a booklet about India's past, present, and future.

**His comparison of Chinese and Indian entrepreneurs:** Balram mentions that the radio announcer explained that the **Chinese are far ahead of India in every respect**, except that the Chinese "don't have entrepreneurs". India, despite having "no drinking water, electricity, sewage system, public transportation, sense of hygiene, discipline, courtesy, or punctuality," does have thousands and thousands of entrepreneurs, especially in the field of technology. He notes that these Indian entrepreneurs "have set up all these outsourcing companies that virtually run America now". He feels good that Premier Jiabao hopes to learn how to make a few Chinese entrepreneurs.

**His description of his office (chandelier, midget fan):** Balram stays up the whole night in his **150-square-foot office**, which he calls "a hole in the wall". There is no one else in the office. Above him is a **chandelier**, which is "a huge thing, full of small diamond-shaped glass pieces," like those shown in 1970s films. He has placed a **midget fan**—with five cobwebby blades—right above the chandelier. When the midget fan turns, the small blades "chop up the chandelier's light and fling it across the room," resembling a strobe light at the best discos in Bangalore. Balram claims this is the "only 150-square-foot space in Bangalore with its own chandelier!". He refers to staying there all night as "The entrepreneur's curse. He has to watch his business all the time".

**His final musings on the 36,000,004 gods:** Balram begins his story by considering which god's "arse" he should kiss, noting that Hindus have **36,000,000 gods**. When added to the one god of the Muslims and the three gods of the Christians, this makes a **grand total of 36,000,004 divine arses** for him to choose from. He notes that some "thinking men" believe that "not many of these gods actually exist" or that "none of them exist," leaving only "us and an ocean of darkness around us". He finds that the gods "seem to do awfully little work—much like our politicians—and yet keep winning reelection". He decides to be "straight and crooked, mocking and believing, sly and sincere, at the same time" (as an Indian entrepreneur must be), and closes his eyes, folds his hands in a namaste, and prays to the gods to shine light on his dark story.

## Plot

**Birth and Childhood in Laxmangarh -**
- Balram Halwai was born into a poor Halwai (sweet-maker) caste family.
- He grows up in Laxmangarh, which is described as a village plagued by poverty, corruption, and landlord oppression.
- Balram Halwai lives in Laxmangarh with his grandmother, parents, brother, and extended family.
- Balram's father dies of TB as there was "no medication" available for him, highlighting that in his village, there is "no law just the way things work". This is a pivotal moment that underscores the poverty and lack of state support.

**The "White Tiger" Nickname and Education:**
- Balram is recognized as a "White Tiger" by a school inspector for his intelligence.
- The title _The White Tiger_ refers to a nickname given to Balram Halwai when he was a child.
- Despite having grown up in extreme poverty, Balram managed to learn to read and answer questions proficiently, which a school inspector recognized as a rarity.
- The inspector referred to Balram as the rarest of animals—a white tiger, a creature that others will notice but can also be incredibly dangerous.
- The inspector promised him a scholarship.

**Leaving School and Working in the Teashop -**
- Balram’s education is cut short.
- His family pulls him out of school.
- They pulled him out of school to work in a tea shop after they fall into debt.
- Working with his brother in a tea shop in Dhanbad which exposes Balram to conversations about Indian government and economy.

**Decision to Learn to Drive -**
- Balram is inspired by Vijay, a fellow villager and former pig herder who becomes a powerful politician.
- Balram learns to drive.
- He secures a job in Dhanbad as the second driver for Mr. Ashok, who is the foreign-educated son of a powerful landlord called the Stork.

**Job as Second Driver in Dhanbad -**
- Balram secures a job in Dhanbad as the second driver for Mr. Ashok.
- Mr. Ashok is the foreign-educated son of a powerful landlord called the Stork.
- Mr. Ashok is recently returned from America.
- Mr. Ashok is married to Pinky Madam, who dislikes India and wants to return to the US.

**Getting Ram Persad Fired and Becoming Primary Driver -**
- Balram blackmails the senior driver, Ram Persad, into leaving.
- Balram did this after discovering Ram Persad is a secret Muslim. Ram Persad hid of being Muslim as he needed to work in a Hindu household, which shows religious discrimination present in the labor market.
- Balram then becomes Mr. Ashok’s primary driver.

**Move to Delhi and Exposure to Corruption -**
- Mr. Ashok moves to Delhi.
- Balram travels from Dhanbad to Delhi (and then to Bangalore later).
- As a chauffeur to Ashok and his wife Pinky Madam, Balram is witness to urban life, corruption in Indian society and the government.
- In Delhi, Balram witnesses elite lifestyles and political corruption.
- He sees this corruption through his master’s involvement in bribing politicians.
- Balram's stay in Delhi occurs in a setting that represents moral decay.
- Balram becomes increasingly corrupt, mirroring his master’s behavior.

**The Hit-and-Run Incident -**
- One night, Pinky Madam, who is drunk, forces Balram to let her drive.
- Pinky Madam accidentally kills a child in a hit-and-run accident.
- Balram is forced to sign a confession, taking the blame for the accident.
- However, no charges are filed. Even if police know something has happened, they won't do anything till someone else files a complaint. This underscores that justice is transactional and passive, rather than active.
- Following this incident, Pinky abruptly leaves for America, abandoning Mr. Ashok.
- Mr. Ashok then spirals into drinking and womanizing.

**The Rooster Coop and Planning Murder -**
- Balram realizes he will never escape servitude unless he takes drastic action.
- He compares himself to a chicken in a Rooster Coop, trapped in lifelong servitude.
- The Rooster Coop symbolizes the poor’s / serving class acceptance of servitude due to family and societal conditioning. They watch others being slaughtered but don't rebel. 
- The novel argues that only someone willing to sacrifice their family can break free—which Balram ultimately does.
- Balram begins contemplating murder.
- He observes Mr. Ashok frequently carrying a red bag filled with bribe money.
- On an isolated street, Balram fakes a car breakdown, lures Mr. Ashok out, and brutally murders him with a broken whiskey bottle. 
- He steals the bag containing 700,000 rupees and flees with his younger cousin, Dharam. This is a crucial plot point because it complicates his escape. He isn't just running away alone; he has a family member with him, which ties back to the theme of family weighing him down.
- Balram refers to this murder as an act of entrepreneurship.

**Resolution: Reinvention in Bangalore -**
- Balram begins a new life in Bangalore, which symbolizes opportunity.
- He changes his name to "Ashok Sharma" to erase his past. This transformation from Munna → Balram Halwai → The White Tiger → Ashok Sharma represents social mobility through deception.
- He uses the stolen money to bribe police officers.
- He then establishes "White Tiger Drivers," a successful taxi service for call center employees.
- Balram, despite his impoverished background, escapes the confines of rural India and a deprived childhood to make his way into the 'Light'.
- Balram becomes corrupt himself, mirroring the corrupt practices of his former employers. He bribes officials to eliminate competition.

## Narrative Style and Structure

**The First-Person Point of View -**
- The novel is told from the perspective of Balram Halwai, the protagonist.
- It utilizes a first-person point of view.
- Balram's letters are narrated by "The White Tiger," A Thinking Man, And an Entrepreneur.
- This perspective allows Balram to offer a monologue about his life.
- The narrative structure parodies the "ancient and venerated custom" of Indian storytelling where the author starts by praying to a Higher Power or Muse. Balram mocks this tradition by asking "Which god's arse, though?" and eventually deciding to pray to all 36,000,004 of them just to be safe . This establishes the irreverent tone immediately.

**The Epistolary Structure (Letters to Wen Jiabao) -**
- The novel is an epistolary novel.
- It is structured as a series of letters from Balram.
- Balram's narrative style as trolling India's official narrative of saintliness and morality. It serves as a satirical counter-narrative to the image projected by leaders like Gandhi or the Prime Minister.
- Balram directs these letters to His Excellency Wen Jiabao, the Chinese Premier or Prime Minister from 2003 to 2013.
- The purpose of this structure is for Balram to tell his life's story and the truth about Bangalore.
- Balram decides to dictate the letter after the lady on All India Radio announced that Premier Jiabao was coming to Bangalore next week.
- The epistolary structure creates a satirical counternarrative to India's official image.

**The Mix of Past and Present Tenses -**
- The novel is written in a mixture of present and past tenses.
- This mixing of tenses covers many events in both the distant and recent pasts.
- It also includes the protagonist's current thoughts. For example, Balram details his past experience as a servant while frequently pausing to address the Premier in the present moment, such as describing the chandelier and midget fan in his office or stating his decision to start dictating the letter.

**The Concept of Balram being an 'Unreliable Narrator' -**
- Balram is characterized as an unreliable narrator.
- As an unreliable narrator, he engages in exaggerating and distorting events for his own justification.
- The novel utilizes this unreliability as part of its satirical critique.
- Balram's status as a self-taught entrepreneur who murdered his former employer and stole money to start his business highlights the moral ambiguity underlying his narrative.
- He tells Premier Jiabao that he wants to be "straight and crooked, mocking and believing, sly and sincere, at the same time", acknowledging his duality, which suggests his account should be taken with skepticism regarding its "sincerity" or "straight" truth.

### Caste System
#### Balram's Rejection of the Traditional Caste System
- Balram rejects the caste system, which traditionally dictated one’s profession and social status.
- Balram was born into a poor Halwai (sweet-maker) caste family.
- His rejection of caste norms reflects the struggle for individual agency in modern India.

- Balram claims that modern India now has only two castes - men with big bellies (the rich) and men with small bellies (the poor, who serve them).
- The novel exposes class struggle and inequality in contemporary India.
- India's economic liberalization and reforms in 1991 led to rapid urbanization, but wealth remained concentrated, deepening the divide between rich and poor.

### Chandeliers
- The Chandeliers are a symbol of the India of light.
- They represent wealth and social mobility.
- They also represent corruption, as Balram obtains them through theft and deception.
- Balram has a chandelier in his 150-square-foot office in Bangalore.
- He describes his office as the only 150-square-foot space in Bangalore with its own chandelier.
- The chandeliers help Balram feel a part of the Light, an assurance he has left the Darkness of his old life and identity behind.
- Balram directly links the chandelier to his lack of family, stating: “I’ve got no family anymore. All I've got is chandeliers”.

### City Symbolism

#### Delhi (Moral Decay)
- Delhi represents moral decay.
- In Delhi, Balram becomes increasingly corrupt, mirroring his master’s behavior.
- Balram travels from Dhanbad to Delhi and then to Bangalore. 
#### Bangalore (Opportunity):
- Bangalore symbolizes opportunity.
- It is a place where Balram reinvents himself as a businessman.
- Balram begins a new life and sets up his successful taxi service, White Tiger Drivers, in Bangalore.
- Balram refers to Bangalore as the world's center of Technology and Outsourcing.
- Bangalore doesn't control India like Delhi. This clarifies why Balram feels safer in Bangalore; Delhi is the center of political power (and thus danger), while Bangalore is the "nervous center" of business but lacks the same stifling political control. 

### Animal Imagery

The novel's use of animal imagery (like the Rooster Coop) to George Orwell and his book Animal Farm. This frames the novel as a "political allegory"2, elevating it beyond just a story about a driver.

#### The White Tiger (Uniqueness and Ambition)
The central symbol of the novel is the White Tiger, which represents Balram’s identity as a rare and dangerous exception to the social order.
- **Origin -** The name is given to Balram by a school inspector who notices his intelligence and proficiency in reading. The inspector calls him the rarest of animals, a white tiger.
- **Symbolism -**
    - **Rarity -** It symbolizes a creature that comes along only once in a generation. In a sea of poverty where millions are trapped, Balram is the unique individual who possesses the cleverness and ambition to escape.
    - **Danger -** The metaphor also carries an element of threat. The inspector notes that the white tiger is a creature others will notice but can also be incredibly dangerous. This foreshadows Balram’s ruthless path to success, including the murder of his employer.
    - **Freedom -** Balram adopts this moniker to signify his transformation from a servant into a free thinking man and entrepreneur.

#### The Rooster Coop (Internalized Servitude)
The Rooster Coop is the novel’s most significant metaphor for the Indian class system and the psychology of the poor.
- **The Metaphor -** Balram describes the millions of Indian servants as chickens trapped in a coop. They can smell the blood of their kin and see them being slaughtered, yet they do not rebel or try to escape.
- **Psychological Entrapment -** The coop does not rely on physical fences but on internalized servitude. The poor are conditioned to accept their lot in life due to family loyalty and societal pressure.
- **Breaking Free -** The novel argues that the coop is guarded by the servants' own love for their families. To break out of the coop—to become a White Tiger—one must be willing to become a social outcast and sacrifice their family, knowing they will likely be punished for the escapee's actions.

#### The Landlords (Predatory Animals)
The feudal landlords who dominate Balram's village of Laxmangarh are given animal names to symbolize their predatory and inhuman nature.
- **The Animals -** The landlords are referred to as the stork, wild boar, buffalo, and raven.
- **The Stork -** This is the nickname for Mr. Ashok’s father, a powerful landlord who collects cuts from the villagers.
- **Dehumanization -** These names serve to dehumanize the rich, portraying them not as people but as beasts who feed on the poor. They represent a food chain where the strong literally consume the resources and lives of the weak.

#### Other Animal Metaphors

- **The Donkey -** This animal is used to symbolize the beast of burden, representing the dehumanized state of the working poor who labor without reward.
- **Worm’s Eye View -** While not a character metaphor, Adiga describes the novel’s perspective as a worm’s eye view. This emphasizes a perspective from the substratum or underbelly of society—looking up at the light from the dirt, rather than looking down from above.

### Corruption and Politics

- **Pervasiveness of Corruption -** The novel reflects the pervasive bribery and nepotism in Indian governance. Balram's journey, even as a chauffeur, makes him a witness to urban life, corruption in Indian society and the government. Balram critiques the moral and saintly image of India presented by its leaders, calling it a joke and exposing corruption, poverty, and servitude. The narrative emphasizes that Balram realizes the stranglehold of an all-encompassing corruption in India.
- **Balram's Own Corruption -** Balram himself eventually mirrors the corrupt practices of his former employers, bribing officials to eliminate competition. His successful business is established using stolen money to bribe police officers. His story suggests that breaking the law is the only way to succeed in a corrupt system.

#### The Illusion of Democracy
- The novel satirizes Indian democracy, showing how the poor have no real political power.
- Indian democracy is portrayed as farcical, controlled by corrupt politicians & feudal lords.
- Elections are rigged, votes are bought, and the police serve the wealthy.
- Balram notes that his country's gods seem to do awfully little work—much like his politicians—and yet keep winning reelection to their golden thrones in heaven, year after year.

#### The Role of Bribery
- Balram witnesses elite lifestyles and political corruption through his master’s involvement in bribing politicians.
- Mr. Ashok, despite his foreign education, actively participates in bribery to maintain his status.
- The plot's turning point involves Mr. Ashok frequently carrying a red bag filled with bribe money, which Balram observes before deciding to murder him and steal the 700,000 rupees.

#### The Great Socialist
- The character of The Great Socialist (a prominent, populist, and corrupt leader) represents political hypocrisy.
-  claiming to uplift the poor while exploiting them.
- The class divide is maintained by figures like The Great Socialist (a prominent, populist leader). 
- This character is described as a hypocrite who spouts nonsense about equality and claims to champion the poor while actually exploiting them. This explains why the Rooster Coop persists politically—the leaders who claim to help the small bellies are actually working against them.

#### Critique of Economic Liberalization and Globalization
- The novel critiques how globalization benefits only a fraction of society.
- India's economic reforms in 1991 led to rapid urbanization and outsourcing jobs.
- However, wealth remained concentrated, deepening the divide between rich and poor.
- Balram notes that India, despite having no drinking water, electricity, sewage system, public transportation, sense of hygiene, discipline, courtesy, or punctuality, does have thousands and thousands of entrepreneurs, especially in the field of technology, who have set up outsourcing companies that virtually run America now. Balram's cynical adoption of this entrepreneurship (murder) suggests that the success of globalization rests on unethical foundations.

#### The Political Class
- The political class is united in their exploitation of the poor.
- Even though politicians appear to be in different parties or have contesting ideologies, the notes argue that they help each other behind the scenes. This reinforces the idea that the poor have no real options.
- Beyond just being corrupt or hypocritical, Indian politics is a criminal network of sorts. Crime isn't just a byproduct of the system, but the structure of the system itself.
